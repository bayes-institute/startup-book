# Chapter 5 — The Theory of Markets and Opportunity Selection

## How to discover where valuable demand exists, where profits can survive, and which opportunity a particular founder should pursue

*Building What Lasts — Part II: Markets, Customers, and Differentiation*

**Edition:** 25 October 2026  
**Audience:** First-time founders, experienced entrepreneurs, investors, operators, and builders of enduring institutions  
**Scope:** Market definition, demand, industrial organization, value chains, market and profit sizing, growth, timing, market cartography, competitive mapping, opportunity selection, field investigation, and an extended Bayes Institute application.

> **The central proposition:** A good market is not simply one with many potential customers, fast growth, or weak competition. It is a setting in which a particular group has a consequential job to accomplish, can and will commit resources to accomplishing it, and a particular business can reach them, serve them, and retain sufficient value after competitors, suppliers, and substitutes respond. A good *founding opportunity* adds a further condition: **this founder can earn an initial position before running out of cash, time, or credibility.**

---

## A prologue: the founder with the brilliant course

Imagine two entrepreneurs who can both teach artificial intelligence extremely well. They each have a laptop, several years of relevant experience, and enough savings to experiment. The first opens a spreadsheet containing the reported size of India's education industry, the growth rate of AI spending, and the number of young people who might want a better job. The numbers are enormous. He launches a six-month AI course for everyone who wants to learn AI.

The second founder asks a narrower and more uncomfortable question: *Who, specifically, is already attempting to solve a problem that I can help with, and what happens if they fail to solve it?* She speaks with working engineers, managers, colleges, and people who recently purchased training. Some groups express enthusiasm but will not pay. Others have budgets but are locked into procurement cycles. A few engineers have been assigned to ship AI features, are stuck at evaluation and reliability, and have already spent weekends and personal money looking for help. The second founder designs a paid, limited diagnostic for them. Perhaps she learns the opportunity is real. Perhaps she discovers that the employer, not the engineer, should be the customer.

The first entrepreneur has a market-size slide. The second is beginning to understand a market.

That distinction is the subject of this chapter.

A market is not a number sitting in a research report. It is a system of decisions: people want progress; they compare alternatives; budgets, habits, institutions, and constraints determine whether they act; businesses assemble resources to serve them; bargaining determines who receives the proceeds. Changes in technology, tastes, policy, and relative costs continuously reshape those decisions. Entering a market means inserting a new option into this system, not merely launching a product beside existing products.

If the goal is to make a founder substantially better at deciding what to build, we need to answer several questions in the right order. Where does demand actually come from? Why can a huge market produce miserable profits? Where in a long value chain are the attractive economics? How can we size a market without inventing customers? What does growth tell us, and what does it conceal? How can we discover a market gap, including when nobody is actively searching for our product? When is timing favorable? Finally, how do we compare several plausible opportunities without turning a subjective decision into a pseudo-scientific score?

This chapter builds on four previous conclusions. From Chapter 1: value creation is not the same as value capture. From Chapter 2: the founder begins with a particular bundle of capabilities and constraints. From Chapter 3: ambition and mission orient the search but cannot substitute for market evidence. From Chapter 4: uncertainty must be reduced through decision-relevant experiments rather than confident storytelling. The following investigation brings all four together.

### What this chapter does not try to do

This chapter **selects an opportunity space**. Chapter 6 investigates customers in depth; Chapter 7 chooses a precise beachhead; Chapter 8 develops differentiation and positioning; Chapter 9 tests a proposition and product–market fit. We will introduce each where necessary because market selection without customer behavior is impossible, but we will not pretend that drawing a market map settles the next four chapters.

The distinction between research and synthesis will remain explicit. Established industrial-organization ideas, market research practices, and primary sources appear in the references. The proposed filters, scorecards, and Bayes decision matrices are **managerial reasoning tools**, not scientifically validated predictors of startup success. Except where a source is cited, all numerical examples and market-specific scenarios are deliberately hypothetical; none represents a verified Bayes forecast.

# Part I — What Is a Market?

## 1. A market is a pattern of exchanges, not an industry label

The phrase *education market* can refer to a parent paying for school, a university offering degrees, a software engineer buying a specialized workshop, a government purchasing vocational training, an employer funding staff development, or an institution licensing learning software. They share some language and inputs but they often have different economic buyers, motives, procurement, competitors, legal obligations, revenue cycles, and willingness to pay.

It is therefore possible to be correct that the education industry is enormous and incorrect that an AI course has an attractive market. Conversely, a tiny and obscure activity can contain a profitable opportunity if its buyers experience an urgent, expensive problem and are reachable at sustainable cost.

Define a candidate market provisionally as the intersection of **a buyer, a job or use situation, a set of alternatives, a mechanism of exchange, and a context**. The context may be geographic, professional, regulatory, or technological. A useful sentence is:

> In **[context]**, **[buyer]** repeatedly faces **[situation]**, attempts to accomplish **[job]**, currently chooses among **[alternatives]**, and may transfer **[money, time, authority, attention]** to an option that improves the result.

This is a more useful starting unit than “the global AI market.” The boundaries can be broadened after studying behavior, but they should not begin so broad that every observation becomes compatible with the hypothesis.

## 2. The five different meanings of “market”

Founders often move between five meanings without noticing:

| Meaning | What it actually describes | Typical error |
|---|---|---|
| **Need or problem space** | People who could benefit from solving a problem | Mistaking benefit for purchase intent |
| **Product category** | Existing products sold under a familiar label | Ignoring substitutes outside the category |
| **Spending category** | Money currently spent by some actors | Assuming spending can readily switch to a startup |
| **Reachable buyer system** | Buyers the startup can economically access and serve | Assuming national demand is reachable from a landing page |
| **Economic opportunity** | Transactions that can produce sustainable contribution and attractive returns | Counting gross purchases instead of retained economics |

A need may be large while spending is negligible. Spending can be large while value capture is poor. A customer segment can be excited but inaccessible. A founder can be able to serve it while incapable of acquiring it. Our analysis must keep the layers separate.

## 3. Buyers, users, payers, influencers, and beneficiaries

In a straightforward consumer transaction, the person choosing, paying, and using a product may be the same. In complex markets they are different.

A student uses a learning program. A college department might select it. An administrator signs the agreement. A finance office approves payment. Faculty determine whether it works operationally. Students' future employers may benefit without paying the college. In enterprise AI training, the sponsor may care about delivery time, the employee about career development, the security team about data handling, and procurement about contract terms.

If the beneficiary has no money, the payer has no incentive, or the buyer lacks authority, a pressing need may remain commercially unserved. This is not a sales-copy issue. It is a market-design issue.

For each market, identify **who suffers the cost**, **who benefits from improvement**, **who controls resources**, **who can stop the transaction**, and **who is accountable afterward**. These actors do not need identical motivations, but the transaction must satisfy enough of them simultaneously.

## 4. Jobs, occasions, and budgets are three different boundaries

A demographic cohort is often too blunt. “Indian adults aged 21–30 interested in AI” might contain casual explorers, working software developers, junior analysts, job seekers, students who cannot afford tuition, and employees funded by a company. These people are not one commercial market merely because their age and interests overlap.

A **job** explains what progress they seek. An **occasion or trigger** explains when they become likely to act. A **budget** explains which resource can fund the action. The most useful market definition usually describes all three: *engineers responsible for shipping a customer-facing AI feature in the next quarter, who lack reliable review capability, and have access either to a personal learning budget or a willing employer sponsor.*

Different jobs can share the same buyer; the same job can be purchased through several budgets. An enterprise IT manager buying mandatory compliance training and the same manager taking a personal statistics course are participating in different purchase systems.

## 5. The relevant market is defined by substitutability, not your website category

Imagine a new logistics software company. Its market is not just “software sold by logistics-software vendors.” If operators can satisfy the same need with spreadsheets, temporary labor, outsourced dispatch, or extra inventory, those substitutes constrain adoption and pricing.

This suggests a practical counterfactual: **If every seller in the named product category disappeared, what would customers actually do?** That reveals the underlying demand and its substitutes. In industrial economics, relevant-market analysis asks how closely alternatives constrain one another; formal competition authorities may use variants of hypothetical-monopolist or small-significant-nontransitory-price-increase tests in appropriate contexts. A startup need not perform legal antitrust analysis, but it benefits from the underlying intuition: a market boundary is meaningful only to the extent that customers would switch between the options inside it and not readily to options outside it.

Two teachers publishing AI courses may not compete directly if one serves research mathematicians and the other trains nontechnical managers. A university's degree can compete with a bootcamp for some career decisions and be categorically different for admission requirements, credential recognition, and social status. **Commercial competitors are defined by the buyer's choice, not by proximity on a directory page.**

## 6. Markets are nested, and the unit of analysis changes with the question

The market for an engineering course can be nested inside professional education, technology training, continuing education, and lifelong learning. Each boundary answers a different question. A broad boundary is useful for tracking policy and long-run opportunity; a narrow boundary is essential for predicting first purchases and delivery economics.

A practical hierarchy:

1. **Macro environment:** demographic, technological, economic, regulatory and cultural changes.
2. **Industry or value system:** organizations that produce, distribute and finance related solutions.
3. **Strategic group:** firms using comparable business models and serving similar needs.
4. **Buyer segment:** people or organizations with common constraints and criteria.
5. **Purchase episode:** a particular buyer confronting a particular event.
6. **Atomic transaction:** the exact exchange the founder can attempt now.

Do not extrapolate evidence from one level to another without a mechanism. Growth in AI infrastructure spend does not mechanically predict spending on instructor-led professional courses. College enrollment counts do not mechanically predict outsourcing budgets. A one-off purchase from a friend does not establish a large, repeatable segment.

## 7. Markets are not discovered all at once

The founder begins with an **idea-space map**, proceeds to a **market hypothesis**, then learns an increasingly precise **buyer situation**, and eventually identifies a **repeatable commercial transaction**. These are stages of knowledge, not bureaucratic approvals.

At first, it is entirely reasonable to hold multiple candidate markets. The danger is treating a broad domain of personal interest as though it were already a set of paying customers. Chapters 2 and 3 help identify where the founder may have an edge and what the organization hopes to become; Chapter 5 asks where enough corresponding demand and profit potential might exist to justify a test.

# Part II — The Economics of Demand

## 8. Need is not demand

A person may need better healthcare, better technical skills, better financial planning, or better transportation and nevertheless purchase nothing. Economic demand requires more than a beneficial outcome. At a specified price and under actual circumstances, some people must be **willing and able to buy**, and the offer must be reachable and credible enough to enter their choice set.

This distinction is essential when the founder cares about a socially valuable mission. Untapped human potential is a real human concern; it is not automatically a market in which a self-financing school can sell any course to anyone. A market has to explain who commits resources, why, and what triggers the commitment. Otherwise it is a worthy problem rather than yet a demonstrated commercial opportunity.

The reverse also occurs: people willingly pay for products that create little lasting benefit. Demand can be driven by identity, novelty, habit, social signaling, belief, and misinformation. A founder evaluating a market should distinguish **commercial demand**, **real customer value**, and **social value**. An enduring institution must consider all three, especially in education.

## 9. The demand chain: from problem to purchase

In a new market, imagine a sequence:

**Underlying need → problem recognition → urgency or occasion → awareness of alternatives → credible expected improvement → affordability and authority → commitment → experienced value → future purchase or recommendation.**

Each link can be weak even if the original need is strong. A technical professional may perceive career risk but not believe additional training will change anything. A department head may value faculty development but have no discretionary budget this semester. A consumer may love a new food product but never find it in a nearby shop. A company may believe a tool saves money but be unable to pass a security review.

The decision to enter a market is therefore a decision to confront its **dominant demand friction**. Which link is currently most expensive to repair? Can a tiny entrant repair it more economically than an incumbent? Are the costs of creating demand likely to exceed the value captured from satisfying it?

## 10. Search, latent, suppressed, derived, and manufactured demand

It helps to separate distinct demand states:

**Active demand:** customers already search for a recognizable solution and compare options. These buyers are easier to identify but often face many competing offers.

**Latent demand:** a costly job exists but buyers have not identified a feasible product. A demonstration or changed technology may turn the latent problem into a purchased category.

**Suppressed demand:** buyers want a result but adoption is constrained by price, access, approval, infrastructure, regulation, or inability to trust suppliers. Lowering a binding constraint may unlock transactions without changing the underlying need.

**Derived demand:** the customer's need arises because another market needs an output. Firms train employees partly because technologies and customer requirements demand capability; specialized components are purchased because downstream manufacturers need them. Derived demand can be volatile if the downstream industry shifts.

**Stimulated or induced demand:** messaging, fashion, new possibilities or policy changes cause people to value a category more. This can be legitimate, but it is dangerous to confuse a short-lived attention spike with durable behavior.

A founder's experiment should match the state. Active-demand markets may need a competitive offer and access. Latent-demand markets may need demonstration and a new purchasing frame. Suppressed-demand markets may need financing, compliance, or distribution changes. Derived-demand markets require studying the downstream buyer, not just the immediate customer.

## 11. Demand is an alternative-dependent choice

A customer rarely asks whether your product has positive value in isolation. They ask whether it improves their situation sufficiently relative to a feasible alternative. One useful conceptual representation is:

\[
\text{Net preference for option }j = \text{expected functional, emotional and social gains} - \text{price, effort, risk and switching burden}.
\]

Do not add arbitrary scores and treat the result as a measured utility function; it is an inventory of causal influences. An enterprise might value reduced operational risk more than speed. An inexperienced learner might value a credible credential more than technical feedback. A busy engineer might prefer a shorter, harder studio to a comprehensive video library. Different segments have different preference functions.

The market-selection implication is that you should not count everybody who *could use* your product. Count people who, under a realistic comparison, might choose it.

## 12. The four degrees of demand evidence

When choosing a market, evidence commonly appears in an ascending sequence:

1. **Expression:** people describe a need, complaint or desire. Useful for vocabulary; weak for quantifying transactions.
2. **Sacrifice already observed:** buyers spend money, staff time, effort or reputation on alternatives or improvised workarounds. Stronger evidence of priority.
3. **Commitment to your proposed class of solution:** they take a relevant pilot, secure a budget, complete procurement work or pay for a bounded product. Evidence of behavior, but possibly atypical.
4. **Repeatable and economically viable purchase:** distinct customers pay under comparable conditions, receive value, and produce enough contribution to finance continued delivery. Much stronger evidence of a business.

The ladder is not a universal measurement scale. A single enterprise contract may be powerful evidence of one client's need and weak evidence of repeatability. A high-frequency FMCG product can prove repeat preference quickly; a research-intensive medical device may require years of validation before meaningful adoption.

A founder should record what the evidence supports and, equally importantly, what it does not.

## 13. The importance of the trigger

Buying often accelerates when an event changes the cost of inaction: a new regulation, a broken process, a promotion, an audit, a product launch, a hiring requirement, a family event, or a change in available technology. *Interest* without a trigger can remain dormant for years.

Ask prospective buyers what happened immediately before their last comparable purchase. Was there a deadline, an embarrassment, a manager request, a friend’s recommendation, or repeated failure? Did they search for months, or buy in a day? How many people were involved? Did the urgency recur?

An attractive entry market often contains **identifiable purchasing moments**, not merely a large population with a vague aspiration. This observation informs distribution later, but it belongs here because some problems are far easier to monetize than others.

## 14. Intensity, frequency, and population multiply in different ways

A market with ten million people who feel mild curiosity may be commercially weaker than one with ten thousand organizations that repeatedly experience expensive failure. However, small intense markets can have hard scale ceilings. Think in terms of three separate quantities:

- **Reachable buyers:** how many independently budgeted decision units exist?
- **Purchase frequency or economic duration:** how often do they commit, or how long does the service relationship last?
- **Contribution potential:** how much value can the seller retain per transaction after full costs?

The expected spending available to a particular category depends on transaction frequency and price; the attractiveness to the entrant depends on **retained profit, required capital, and acquisition feasibility**. An infrequent high-stakes sale may require heavy trust-building. A frequent low-price product may depend on physical distribution. Neither has a universally superior structure.

## 15. Price elasticity, substitutes, and the size of the opportunity

Price is not external to the size of a market. Quantity purchased usually changes with price, offer attributes, trust, and competitors' responses. A frequently quoted market value is therefore a historical description of spending at prevailing conditions, not a fixed pool of money that every entrant can share.

In a simplified single-product context, the **price elasticity of demand** is

\[
\varepsilon = \frac{\%\text{ change in quantity demanded}}{\%\text{ change in price}}.
\]

Actual elasticity can vary dramatically across customer groups, quantities, time periods and substitutes. The important selection question is not simply whether buyers have money, but **how strongly they can bargain, switch, postpone, self-produce or forego the result**. A valuable but easily replicated service may face fierce price pressure; a modest saving embedded in a critical process may be relatively insensitive to price, provided buyer trust and reliability requirements are met.

## 16. Demand can grow while willingness to pay declines

Technology often increases consumption and reduces price simultaneously. Cheaper model inference may allow far more AI-enabled tasks while making standalone generic AI functionality less valuable per task. An abundance of free educational content may increase the number of people learning while reducing the price anyone will pay for video lectures. A growing market can therefore produce expanding units, flat revenue, and shrinking profit per provider.

Separate **growth in problems**, **growth in adoption**, **growth in paid transactions**, **growth in realized prices**, and **growth in supplier profit**. A report that says “AI adoption is exploding” does not tell you which of these is increasing for a narrow professional education product.

## 17. The demand ceiling and the adoption ceiling are different

A product might be desirable to almost everyone but adopted by very few because onboarding, procurement, geography, compatibility or trust is the limiting factor. Conversely, it might be easy to adopt but serve a small, infrequent need.

The founder must estimate two different ceilings: the number of people who **could rationally benefit** and the number who **can plausibly be reached, persuaded, onboarded and served** at acceptable economics. The latter is the near-term commercial ceiling. A new venture dies by confusing the former with the latter.

## 18. Demand is partly endogenous to what you build

The market is not always an unchanging substance waiting to be mined. Better products can create new use cases, reduce the learning required for adoption, expand the range of paying customers, or change who pays. The history of consumer computing, software-as-a-service, and many accessibility technologies demonstrates the general logic that lowering friction can enlarge participation.

This is a reason not to rely exclusively on current spending. But it is also why a market-size forecast is uncertain: it embeds a **theory of how behavior will change**. Make that theory explicit. Which barrier disappears? Which new buyer becomes able to act? Why will somebody pay rather than merely benefit? Which adjacent markets must cooperate? A plausible expansion mechanism is more important than a beautiful compound annual growth curve.

# Part III — Market Structures and the Division of Economic Value

## 19. The question economics asks that founders often forget

A founder sees customers paying ₹100 crore a year and thinks: *There must be a lot of money to earn.* An economist asks: **After a capable supplier enters, who can keep any of that money?**

Revenue flowing through a category is distributed among workers, suppliers, intermediaries, capital providers, tax authorities, customers, and businesses competing for the transaction. Some industries create immense consumer benefit yet allow little sustained excess profit for most suppliers. Others permit firms to retain meaningful margins because they possess scarce rights, strong customer preference, differentiated capabilities, economies of scale, regulated infrastructure, or control of valuable bottlenecks.

An opportunity must be evaluated on two dimensions: **the amount of value customers seek** and **the economic structure governing how much a provider can retain**. Confusing them is one of the most expensive mistakes in business selection.

## 20. Perfect competition is a reference model, not a description of most startups

In the textbook model of perfect competition, many sellers supply an essentially interchangeable product; buyers have good information; entry and exit are comparatively easy; no firm individually sets the market price; and in long-run equilibrium economic profits are competed away. Real markets rarely meet all assumptions, yet the model explains what happens as offerings become interchangeable and switching becomes easy.

If one seller of a standard input charges substantially more for identical quality, delivery, financing and assurance, buyers defect. The rational entry strategy may then be superior cost, logistics, location, specialized service, or avoiding the market. Attractive demand does not create the right to earn an attractive markup.

**Economic profit** here means profit after accounting for an appropriate opportunity cost of capital and resources, not just positive accounting net income. A profitable small business can offer the owner a desirable livelihood without generating persistent economic rents.

## 21. Monopolistic competition: differentiation with continual entry

Restaurants, consumer brands, agencies, and many short-course providers are closer to **monopolistic competition**: many firms sell products that are similar but not identical. Each can enjoy some customer preference, yet new entrants and substitutes limit the premium.

This market can be good for a specialist and poor for an undifferentiated generalist. A bakery can serve a neighborhood exceptionally well. A boutique technical trainer can be preferred by a particular professional group. Neither needs a national monopoly. But as profitable positions become visible, others enter, imitating attractive elements and competing for customer attention. The founder must assess whether their advantages will be enough to support repeat contribution after the initial novelty fades.

## 22. Oligopoly: few rivals do not mean easy entry

An **oligopoly** has a small number of consequential competitors whose decisions affect one another. Airlines on a route, telecom networks, semiconductor fabrication, digital infrastructure, and segments of industrial manufacturing may display oligopolistic features. Entry may be expensive because of technology, permits, switching costs, scarce assets, network scale or a history of competitive pricing.

A market with just three incumbents might appear uncrowded. It can actually be the worst possible place for a cash-constrained founder if each incumbent has enormous capital, entrenched distribution, and incentives to counterattack. Conversely, there may be neglected customer groups the incumbents cannot serve profitably under their current models.

Evaluate **why there are few competitors**. Is it because the market is unattractive, the minimum efficient scale is high, regulatory access is scarce, or a particular provider has already solved the distribution problem? Lack of competition is a diagnostic question, not a green flag.

## 23. Monopoly and monopsony: power exists on both sides

A monopoly has unusually strong seller-side market power over a relevant product or geographic market, though even monopolists face substitutes and constraints. A **monopsony** has unusually strong buyer-side power. In practical B2B markets, a small number of large buyers may capture much of the surplus from many suppliers even without a literal single buyer.

Suppose a startup produces a specialized component needed by three giant customers. Even if demand is technically strong, customers may demand severe price concessions, customized terms, long credit periods, or exclusivity. A service firm with one college accounting for 80% of revenue may have a customer-concentration problem no matter how impressed that college is with the work.

Before entering a market, assess **the balance of bargaining power**: how much each side loses if a transaction fails, how easily it can replace the other side, and who has better outside options.

## 24. Two-sided and multisided markets: one market may need another

Marketplaces, payment networks, advertising platforms, developer ecosystems, app stores, and some talent platforms require participation from multiple groups. Each side's willingness to join may depend on the quality and quantity of the other sides. This can create network effects, but also a severe cold-start problem.

A new tutor marketplace with thousands of registered tutors and almost no students is not yet liquid. A talent platform with many students and no real employer demand is not automatically valuable. The relevant unit is often **successful matches in a narrow atomic market**, not registrations.

The founder must estimate acquisition and incentives for **all indispensable sides**, how transactions are priced, who can bypass the platform, and where matching density can be reached economically. One side may need temporary subsidy; subsidies are sound only if they generate measurable value for a paying side, not because “network effects” looks good in a pitch deck.

## 25. Regulated, natural-monopoly, and infrastructure markets

Where fixed costs are immense relative to demand, duplicating a network may be wasteful; a **natural-monopoly** structure can arise over a relevant scale or geography. Utilities, some transportation infrastructure, and certain communication networks have historically exhibited related economics, often with regulatory oversight.

For founders, the lesson is not that these markets are impossible. It is that entry may occur at another layer: inspection, maintenance, specialized equipment, analytics, financing, energy efficiency, compliance, or niche services that complement the incumbent infrastructure. The attractive location in the value chain may not be the largest or most visible asset.

Regulation can create demand and defensibility, but can also impose capital requirements, licensing expenses, enforcement risk, and restrictions on pricing or claims. Never count a regulated activity as an accessible market without verifying authorization, procurement eligibility, and the true time to approval.

## 26. A market structure is a spectrum, not a permanent label

The same industry can contain commodity elements, monopolistic differentiation, concentrated platforms, and local oligopolies. In education, free information may approach commodity conditions, individual expert instruction may be differentiated, formal degrees may be regulated and reputation-sensitive, and an employer-specific training contract may be governed by bilateral bargaining.

A company can migrate between structures. A platform may initially face substitutes and later acquire network power. A once-proprietary software feature may become commoditized through open source or model improvements. An exclusive distribution relationship may expire. A local service niche can become crowded as successful providers attract imitation.

Do not classify “AI education” as though it has one competition regime. Analyze **the precise transaction and bottleneck** under investigation.

## 27. The five forces: look beyond rivals

Michael Porter's five-forces framework asks about **rivalry among existing competitors**, **threat of new entry**, **substitutes**, **buyer power**, and **supplier power**. The framework is useful not because it guarantees an answer but because it forces attention away from a superficial competitor list and toward the mechanisms distributing the industry's economic surplus [R1].

For a market candidate, answer the following with evidence:

| Force | What strengthens it? | Market-entry question |
|---|---|---|
| Rivalry | Many comparable sellers, slow growth, high fixed costs, low switching, difficult exit | Do firms win mainly by discounting or buying customers? |
| New entrants | Low start-up cost, easy copying, accessible channels | Will a successful niche attract ten imitators quickly? |
| Substitutes | Similar outcomes via different products, self-production or inaction | What is the next-best *way* to solve the job? |
| Buyer power | Concentrated buyers, transparent comparison, low switching, backward integration | Can buyers force our margin near cost? |
| Supplier power | Scarce talent, platforms, IP, distribution, equipment, finance | Can suppliers capture the benefits of our success? |

Avoid mechanically rating every force “high,” “medium,” or “low.” Identify **specific causes, relative bargaining options, observable manifestations and potential change**. Buyer power in a paid public workshop differs from buyer power in university procurement even when both concern technical teaching.

## 28. Rivalry is about the mode of competition, not just competitor count

A market with twenty competitors can support healthy specialist firms if customers value different things and can find the right match. A market with four firms can have destructive price wars if providers carry heavy fixed costs and products are interchangeable. Industry *concentration* and *rivalry intensity* are related but not equivalent.

Study how firms win: price, convenience, innovation, reliability, speed, distribution, proprietary standards, bundling, finance, or status. Observe discount cycles, tender requirements, advertising costs, contract duration, switching behavior, and what purchasers actually compare. **The rules of the contest matter more than the number of names on the field.**

A founder attracted to a “crowded market” should ask whether it is crowded because customer demand is healthy, because entry is too easy, because everyone sells the same undifferentiated promise, or because distribution channels subsidize endless introductions. These situations imply different strategies.

## 29. Growth can make rivalry worse

Suppose a market grows 25% annually. A founder imagines that everyone can expand without competing. But rapid growth can summon venture funding, established distributors, suppliers that vertically integrate, large platforms that bundle the product, and opportunistic entrants. If entry costs fall even faster than demand grows, profits may decline while sales volume rises.

Conversely, a slow-growing niche may support excellent economics through deep specialization, recurring clients, and lower customer-acquisition costs. Growth is important, but **industry growth is not the same as profit growth, and neither is the same as the entrant's growth**.

## 30. The incumbent response is an input, not an afterthought

Market-size decks often assume the entrant wins market share in a static environment. Real incumbents change prices, bundle services, improve products, offer exclusive agreements, lobby for lawful regulatory changes, recruit talent, imitate useful ideas, or acquire challengers.

For each promising opportunity, construct three rival reactions:

**Ignore:** the entrant's segment is unattractive or non-threatening under the incumbent model.  
**Match:** the incumbent copies the offer at comparable economics.  
**Bypass:** the incumbent or a substitute solves the underlying job in a different, cheaper way.

Then ask if the new business survives the plausible response. An apparent gap can exist because solving it would damage an incumbent's margins; this can be an opportunity, especially when the entrant has a different cost structure. But do not assume incumbents are incapable of adaptation. The founder's strategic thesis must explain *why* the opening can persist long enough to build a real business.

## 31. Complementors deserve a separate investigation

Porter's five forces are a strong industry-structure lens; platforms and modern technological ecosystems also make **complements** especially important. A complement increases the value of another product: an operating system and compatible applications, a payment network and merchants, a learning program and authentic project opportunities, an EV and charging infrastructure.

A complement can expand the amount of value the system creates and then bargain for much of that value. A software startup relying entirely on a model-provider API may see margins compressed by API pricing or bundling. An educational institution whose premium depends on employers recognizing its assessments may be constrained by those employers' willingness to participate. Brandenburger and Nalebuff's work emphasizes that complementors can shape both market growth and the allocation of gains [R2].

For each market, ask **what else must exist for this product to matter**, who controls it, how expensive it is to access, and whether that partner has incentives aligned with ours.

## 32. Entry barriers and exit barriers are not the same

High entry barriers may protect incumbents but make entry costly for you. Low entry barriers ease your launch but also ease imitation. Exit barriers—non-recoverable investments, long contracts, specialized facilities, staff obligations, and reputational consequences—can keep inefficient rivals in the market, maintaining price pressure.

A rational founder should map barriers in both directions:

- How much cash and elapsed time must be committed **before the first evidence of demand**?
- Which assets can be reused or sold if the thesis fails?
- What fixed commitments remain if revenue disappoints?
- Which capabilities become harder to replicate **after** initial success?

A lean professional service may be attractive because its first test is cheap even if imitation is easy. A large campus may have higher barriers and deeper irreversibility; that does not make it a better *initial* opportunity. **The best entry path is not always through the business layer with the strongest eventual barriers.**

## 33. Structural attractiveness versus attainable superiority

Industry analysis estimates the pressure on the *average* competitor. It does not prove every firm earns the same return. A low-cost producer can prosper in a difficult commodity category. An especially skilled boutique may win in a crowded service market. A highly concentrated industry can be inaccessible to a newcomer.

We need three separate judgments:

1. **Industry economics:** can a well-run company at this layer normally retain value?
2. **Relative position:** could our specific model create a better outcome or lower cost than alternatives?
3. **Entry feasibility:** can we reach a viable position before time and capital run out?

An otherwise attractive industry can fail the third test. A structurally difficult industry may still contain a differentiated micro-market that passes all three. This is why opportunity selection cannot be reduced to finding “the industry with the best margins.”

# Part IV — Value Chains, Bottlenecks, and Profit Pools

## 34. Follow the money through the entire system

People often enter the most visible part of an industry because that is where the end product sits. Yet the best economics may be upstream, downstream, or adjacent. A fashion retailer might own customer attention while manufacturers earn thin margins. A software cloud provider might capture recurring infrastructure economics while a thin reseller competes on price. A college delivering education may spend most of a particular contract fee on faculty and campus operations while a specialist assessment provider retains a different, possibly healthier margin.

The analytical technique is to draw a **value chain**—the sequence of activities through which inputs become a delivered outcome—and then build a **profit-pool map**—an estimate of where revenues, costs, assets and profits actually reside. Gadiesh and Gilbert developed a practical profit-pool lens precisely to counter the temptation to chase large revenue segments without understanding profit distribution [R3].

## 35. A value chain is not only a supplier list

Start with the customer's desired outcome, then work backward through the activities required to produce it. A simplified professional education chain might be:

**Problem recognition → customer acquisition → readiness assessment → curriculum and instructional design → faculty sourcing → learning delivery → practice and feedback → independent assessment → evidence and credentialing where legitimate → learner follow-up → referral or repeat engagement.**

The actors providing these services may include teaching providers, employers, learning platforms, universities, payment providers, marketing agencies, consultants, publishers, cloud platforms, and certification bodies. Several actors can combine activities; some activities can be done in-house or bought externally.

For each step, record not merely *who performs it* but **who is paid, who carries risk, what is scarce, what can be substituted, what accumulates with scale, and what the customer would lose if it failed**.

## 36. Draw three maps, not one

A market becomes much clearer when the founder draws three overlapping maps:

**The value-flow map:** what work creates the result and which participants are required.  
**The money-flow map:** who pays whom, on what terms, with what timing and retained economics.  
**The power-flow map:** who controls demand, scarce inputs, permissions, standards, switching costs and critical data rights.

These maps can disagree. A teacher may create most of the visible educational value while the institution controls distribution and retains the customer relationship. A consumer-goods manufacturer may own production while retailers control shelf access. A promising AI application may create the user-facing experience while a foundation-model platform or enterprise software incumbent holds the decisive distribution power.

It is possible to be centrally involved in **value creation** and poorly positioned for **value capture**. Market entry should avoid that trap where feasible.

## 37. Profit pools need margins and capital, not only revenue shares

For a multi-layer industry, construct a provisional table:

| Layer | Annual revenue attributable to layer | Operating or contribution economics | Capital / working capital required | Power and volatility |
|---|---:|---|---|---|
| Customer acquisition / distribution | Estimate | Commission, CAC, margin | Working capital, brand spend | Algorithm or channel dependence |
| Core delivery / production | Estimate | Labor, variable costs, utilization | Staff, equipment, content | Capacity and quality constraints |
| Software / infrastructure | Estimate | Recurring fees less hosting and support | Product R&D, reliability | Platforms and switching costs |
| Assessment / assurance | Estimate | Verification economics | Expert calibration, systems | Legitimacy and trust |
| Financing / fulfillment | Estimate | Net fee or spread | Credit and liquidity capital | Default and policy risk |

This is a template, not an empirical claim about which layer is most profitable. Also beware double-counting. A final ₹100 customer payment may pass through distributor, manufacturer, input supplier and payment provider; summing each intermediate actor's *gross revenue* can count the same economic activity multiple times. Specify whether you are mapping **gross transactions, value added, or final end-customer spend**. If calculating an industry profit pool, use comparable profit definitions and allocation rules.

## 38. Gross margin, operating margin, cash conversion, and return on capital are different

A market with attractive gross margins can be a bad business if sales and support are expensive. A business with healthy operating profit can face a cash crisis if customers pay after 120 days and payroll is due monthly. A capital-intensive firm can show accounting profits while earning an inadequate return on the plant and working capital invested.

At minimum, investigate:

- **Contribution margin:** revenue less expenses incremental to serving a transaction or customer.
- **Operating profit:** revenue less ongoing operating costs, with consistent definitions.
- **Cash conversion:** when invoices become cash relative to obligations and working capital.
- **Return on invested capital:** whether operating returns justify capital committed, with careful definitions and tax treatment.
- **Founder-hours contribution:** what remains per scarce hour after direct costs, especially in service businesses.

A gigantic market with positive gross margins can be worse than a smaller opportunity that produces dependable cash and reusable capabilities. You cannot assess attractiveness responsibly from one margin percentage.

## 39. Bottlenecks decide who has bargaining power

A **bottleneck** is a constrained, difficult-to-substitute input or activity required to complete the customer's job. A shortage of experienced technical reviewers may be the bottleneck in advanced education. Shelf space may be the bottleneck for a new consumer brand. Security approval may be the bottleneck for enterprise software. Radio spectrum and infrastructure can be bottlenecks for telecom networks. Licenses and specialist trials can be bottlenecks in regulated fields.

Who owns or controls the bottleneck? Can an entrant realistically acquire it? Could the market redesign around it? A scarce bottleneck may support higher economic returns, but scarcity can also raise costs for everyone. Sometimes the correct opportunity is to **remove a bottleneck** for an incumbent rather than compete to own the entire final customer product.

This is a powerful source of business ideas: watch where a growing industry queues, waits, fails quality review, hires expensive specialists, or depends on a tiny number of gatekeepers.

## 40. The make-or-buy boundary reveals opportunities

Every organization decides which activities to do internally and which to purchase. Outsourcing becomes appealing when a specialist can perform a job with better quality, lower total cost, more speed, or less managerial burden than an internal team—while preserving sufficient control, security, integration and accountability.

A college may teach its own courses but source specialized AI instruction, industry projects, faculty development or assessments. A software company may build products internally but hire a consultancy for a difficult model-evaluation audit. A manufacturer may outsource logistics but retain formulation and quality assurance.

The opportunity is often located at a boundary where the current owner is **unwilling or unable to develop specialist capability economically**. But the counterfactual matters: a large customer may choose to bring the function in-house once it becomes strategically important. Examine the customer's internal labor, procurement process, opportunity cost, and willingness to remain dependent on an external provider.

## 41. Changing industry architecture can open new layers

When technology lowers the cost of a formerly scarce component, downstream businesses become possible while existing profit pools shift. Cloud infrastructure made many software products easier to launch without buying servers. Smartphone distribution opened ecosystems for complementary services. Advances in general-purpose AI can reduce the cost of explanation, transcription, drafting and conventional code generation while potentially increasing the importance of evaluation, integration, oversight, authentication and distribution.

These are **conditional mechanisms**, not a claim that all value automatically migrates to the next layer. If validation also becomes commoditized, another bottleneck may matter. What remains scarce depends on how the whole system evolves.

A founder seeking a market gap should ask: **Which input has suddenly become cheap, and which previously uneconomic customer job can now be served?** Then ask: **Who gains bargaining power as a result?** This is more useful than simply repeating that a technology is revolutionary.

## 42. A value-chain adjacency may be a better entry than the final ambition

Suppose your long-term dream is a research institution. You cannot begin with advanced laboratories, a faculty senate, regulatory degree authority, and a large campus on a zero-capital budget. But perhaps the same scientific and instructional capabilities can initially be sold through narrow learning cohorts, faculty training, applied audits, laboratory project services, or enterprise programs.

These are not automatically good businesses. Each must prove demand and healthy economics. Their strategic appeal is that one may produce **cash, instructional evidence, relationships, and skilled personnel** that later help build the broader institution. A different business may generate more cash but few relevant assets. This trade-off belongs in opportunity selection: economic return today, capabilities accumulated tomorrow, and the cost of distraction.

## 43. The rent-capture test

Before selecting an attractive-looking position in a value chain, finish three sentences:

> We create value because **[mechanism]** changes the customer's outcome compared with **[alternative]**.  
> We are paid because **[buyer and budget]** can approve **[transaction]**.  
> We retain enough economics because **[delivery cost, relative scarcity, bargaining and competitive conditions]** do not allow others to take the entire surplus.

If the second or third sentence is vague, you have a solution hypothesis, not yet a business opportunity. If a critical supplier captures nearly all the value, consider a different layer, a different contract, an alternative input or a business that can accumulate more power over time.

# Part V — Market Sizing, Growth, and the Timing of Entry

## 44. The market-size slide is usually the first misleading thing a founder makes

Suppose a report estimates billions in annual spending on technology education. The founder multiplies that figure by 1% and declares a large business opportunity. There are several problems. The category may include degrees, schools, corporate budgets, free government programs, recruitment services, textbooks, and platforms the founder cannot offer. The estimate may include intermediated revenue counted more than once. Spending may take place in countries and organizations the founder cannot reach. Existing customers may be locked into other contracts, may lack reasons to switch, or may want a different outcome. Most importantly, the 1% has no acquisition mechanism.

A market size is useful only if one can explain **the customers, the transactions, the time period, and the assumptions by which the proposed business reaches them**. Big numbers are a poor substitute for causal specificity.

## 45. TAM, SAM, and SOM are different claims

**Total addressable market (TAM):** the total annual spending or economic opportunity for a *clearly defined solution category* if all relevant demand could be served. Definitions differ; a TAM stated in dollars must not conceal incompatible revenue models or geographies.

**Serviceable available market (SAM):** the portion that matches the product's genuine eligibility, delivery capabilities, geography, regulation, language, customer requirements and business model.

**Serviceable obtainable market (SOM):** the portion a particular company could plausibly win in a specified period given reachability, competition, acquisition capacity, sales cycles, cash, product readiness and delivery capacity.

The usual diagram of three nested circles is too comforting. SOM is not discovered by choosing a convenient 1% of SAM. It is estimated from a believable route to actual customers. And TAM may grow or shrink as price, behavior, technology, and category boundaries change.

### The market-sizing identity

For a reasonably defined set of commercial transactions, a useful starting model is:

\[
\text{Annual market spend} \approx \sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(\text{eligible paying buyers}_i\times\text{transactions per buyer per year}_i\times\text{realized price per transaction}_i\bigr).
\]

For subscription services, replace transaction count with paying accounts or seats and a clearly defined annualized revenue measure, taking churn, expansion and billing rules into account. For two-sided platforms, distinguish **gross merchandise value (GMV)** from **platform revenue** (take rate and fees) and still further from contribution. For commodities, use volume, grade and realized price. For services, consider contract duration, average contract value and repeat probabilities. Always state whether taxes collected for the government are excluded.

## 46. Bottom-up sizing begins with the smallest real buyer unit

There are two familiar approaches:

**Top-down:** begin with a published category total and narrow it using segmentation assumptions. Useful for bounds and context; dangerous when categories are mismatched.

**Bottom-up:** count identifiable buyers or demand occasions, estimate plausible participation and spending, and check whether a business can reach that spending. More useful for startup entry because every factor can eventually be tested.

A credible sizing exercise uses **both** and investigates large disagreements. If a report suggests a multibillion-rupee opportunity while the bottom-up map finds only 600 budget holders reachable through your initial channel, either the market definition or your access assumptions need revision. This disagreement is valuable; it identifies what you do not yet understand.

## 47. Worked example: a specialized professional training market

Imagine a hypothetical region containing **60,000 employed technical professionals** in relevant occupations. For the sake of calculation—not because research has proved any of these rates—suppose 20% meet a program's prerequisites and face the relevant job; 25% of that group experience an actionable trigger in a given year; and 30% of those triggered professionals could afford, schedule and seriously consider purchasing a premium instructor-led service. Assume a total realized program fee of ₹40,000.

| Filter | Hypothetical count | Meaning |
|---|---:|---|
| Broad professional population | 60,000 | Background population, **not** paying demand |
| Relevant eligibility (20%) | 12,000 | Appropriate technical preparation and job |
| Annual triggering need (25% of eligible) | 3,000 | Potential purchase occasions in the year |
| Buyer constraints and serious consideration (30%) | 900 | A modeled serviceable demand pool |
| Annual fee for complete offer | ₹40,000 | Hypothetical realized price |
| **Indicative annual serviceable spend** | **₹3.6 crore** | 900 × ₹40,000; *not* Bayes revenue |

The calculation does not say that 900 people will pay Bayes. The 30% is an assumption, not observed conversion; the 900 may include people who choose a competitor or self-study. The annual count may change under marketing, price, and product design. A more rigorous estimate would carry ranges and cross-check against actual recent comparable purchases.

Now suppose the startup's first-year operating plan can realistically contact **180** eligible prospects, qualify **60**, conduct **30** substantive sales conversations, and obtain **12** paid enrollments. The 12 are **a hypothetical acquisition scenario**, not the market's 1% and not a forecast. It produces **₹4.8 lakh in gross tuition**, before any economic costs. If the founder can responsibly supervise only eight learners, even the 12-seat scenario fails a delivery-capacity test.

This is what a useful market-size analysis should accomplish: connect demand to a concrete acquisition and production system.

## 48. Worked example: a college partnership market

The market for a college-supplied AI elective cannot be estimated simply by multiplying India's student population by a course fee. In most such arrangements the **college** is the buyer, students are the users, procurement and academic approval matter, and a contract may be priced per section, semester, program, student, or institution.

Start with **a named list of institutions** in the accessible geography. For each, identify decision-maker, qualifying curriculum need, students or sections affected, outside vendor spending, approval cycle, willingness to outsource, payment terms, timetable, faculty coordination, and technical infrastructure. Then estimate a **contractable** subset rather than counting every college as a prospect.

For example, suppose a founder can identify 40 relevant colleges, 10 of which agree to a serious discussion, 4 request a priced pilot, and 1 eventually signs. At a hypothetical pilot fee of ₹3 lakh, the outcome is ₹3 lakh **booked or contracted** if actually signed, not necessarily cash received or revenue recognized. Fulfillment and collection costs still determine whether the opportunity is attractive.

The question is not how many students exist in India. It is whether **a repeatable college buying mechanism** exists for the specific intervention, and whether institutions will renew after seeing real educational value.

## 49. Worked example: an enterprise AI service

Imagine a specialist firm offering a two-stage evaluation-and-upskilling engagement to product teams. The target might be companies deploying customer-facing AI systems without adequate testing practices. Here the first addressable unit may be a **team with budget authority and a specific deployed or planned workflow**, not a company employee.

A plausible bottom-up model counts target companies × teams with the problem × annual buying incidence × average actual contract value. But these factors are correlated and often overlap. Some large companies centralize all purchasing; others have several independent budgets. A company-level count multiplied by employees can double-count the same training budget. Determine the real procurement unit before estimating spend.

The first-year attainable amount should be built from named accounts, authentic access routes, meetings, evaluations, procurement stages, realistic probability ranges, fulfillment capacity and payment timing. A small founder might have a theoretical market of thousands of teams and be able to sell and deliver only one or two serious engagements per quarter. That constraint is commercially decisive.

## 50. Why market size estimates disagree

Published figures often differ because they measure different things: gross billings versus net platform revenue; formal providers versus informal businesses; current spending versus potential spending; countries with different purchasing power; consumer retail prices versus manufacturer sales; software licenses versus all implementation labor; enrollment counts versus course purchases; forecast periods with different base years.

In practice, examine:

1. **Definition:** Which products, transactions and buyer groups are counted?
2. **Denominator:** People, accounts, seats, firms, devices, locations, or transactions?
3. **Price:** List, realized, retail, wholesale, average, median, gross or net?
4. **Frequency:** One-time, monthly, annual, recurring contract, or replacement cycle?
5. **Geography and eligibility:** Which constraints are assumed away?
6. **Methods:** Surveys, administrative counts, financial statements, industry models or expert estimates?
7. **Date and currency:** What period and exchange assumptions apply?
8. **Uncertainty:** How sensitive is the answer to the weakest factor?

The first purpose of TAM work is **not** to impress investors. It is to expose which assumptions would change a founding decision.

## 51. Use ranges, scenarios, and sensitivity—not a single enormous number

If every factor in a market estimate is uncertain, multiplying point estimates creates an illusion of precision. For the professional cohort example, use conservative, central and expansive scenarios with clear, different assumptions about relevant buyers, trigger incidence, ability to pay and substitute preference. Do not treat these scenarios as statistical confidence intervals unless the inputs actually justify that interpretation.

A **tornado analysis** asks which factor most changes the result. If affordability is the largest uncertainty, investigate prior spending and real payment tests. If trigger incidence matters most, interview recent buyers about timing. If reachability dominates, test specific channels. The model becomes a research agenda.

Also guard against dependency between factors: high willingness to pay may occur in a smaller segment with longer sales cycles; a high price may reduce buyer count; a major competitor's entry may change both conversion and CAC. A simple product of independent probabilities can be misleading.

## 52. Two separate market ceilings: demand and founder throughput

Even if 900 qualified customers could conceivably buy each year, a founder with 20 hours per week and no teaching bench cannot deliver infinite live feedback. The attainable business may be bounded by **expert hours**, not market demand. A SaaS firm may be bounded by procurement and integration capacity. A commodity distributor may be bounded by inventory finance. A food company may be bounded by shelf access and production yield.

Write the first-year ceiling in two ways:

\[
\text{Customers obtainable} \leq \text{qualified prospects reached}\times\text{realistic paid conversion},
\]

and

\[
\text{Customers deliverable} \leq \text{capacity under promised quality and available cash}.
\]

The achievable number is constrained by the smaller of the two, plus any other binding constraints. This is an upper bound under assumed parameters, not a guaranteed outcome. **Market attractiveness for a founder is filtered by what the founder can actually sell and fulfill.**

## 53. The market can be huge and the company can be small—and still excellent

A small fraction of a huge market does not automatically make a large business. Conversely, a specialized firm can build a valuable owner-controlled enterprise in a compact market. Opportunity selection must distinguish the **economic target** from a social convention that every company should pursue venture-scale growth.

If the founder wants a cash-generating specialist practice, a narrow high-margin and repeatable niche might be sufficient. If they want a venture-backed platform, the market must support extremely large scale with economics and distribution suitable for expansion. If they want to bootstrap an institution, the first market may be modest but should have a credible path to accumulating capital, reputation and capabilities for adjacent offerings.

Choose the market for the company you intend to build, not for the largest slide you can draw.

## 54. Market growth has multiple mechanisms

A market can expand because of more buyers, greater frequency, higher prices, greater usage per buyer, replacement of informal work with paid solutions, expansion into new geographies, regulation, complementary infrastructure, or a newly possible product. These sources have different implications for entrants.

For market revenue, a useful decomposition is:

\[
\text{Revenue} = \text{paying customers}\times\text{paid usage per customer}\times\text{realized price per usage unit},
\]

with the terms defined consistently. Growth in one component can be offset by decline in another. If customers are buying more AI-assisted work while unit prices collapse, revenue may grow slower than usage. If an enterprise training budget remains flat but is redistributed from generic awareness training to applied workflow evaluation, the opportunity may arise from **substitution within a budget**, not total spending growth.

## 55. Growth-stage labels are helpful, but the curve is not destiny

Markets are often described as **emergent, growing, maturing, and declining**. The sequence is a pedagogical simplification. Different segments can occupy different stages simultaneously. A broad market may grow while a particular category declines; a mature technology may create a fresh niche in a newly regulated industry.

Still, the stage affects the founder's work:

| Stage | Typical demand situation | Likely founder burden | Primary danger |
|---|---|---|---|
| Emerging | Customer does not recognize the category or fears change | Prove feasibility and educate a narrow buyer | Invented demand; expensive adoption |
| Growing | Need and budgets become visible, many entrants appear | Execute and find a specific position | Overpaying for customer acquisition |
| Maturing | Table stakes and benchmarks standardize | Compete on cost, trust, access, integration or specialization | Feature convergence and price pressure |
| Declining / transforming | Some jobs disappear or move elsewhere | Find underserved residual demand or new uses | Confusing a temporary rebound with recovery |

The stage does not decide whether entry is good. The question is **which forces are changing and where your capabilities help**.

## 56. Diffusion and adoption are not the same as a revenue forecast

New products often show adoption patterns that resemble an S-curve: slow early adoption, acceleration as awareness and access improve, and eventual saturation. Innovation-diffusion models such as the Bass model can describe some categories and are useful for thinking about imitation and external influence [R4]. But a smooth historical S-curve does not establish the future, and fitting one to sparse data can produce misleading certainty.

For a startup, the relevant issue is not predicting a perfect diffusion curve. It is identifying **the next adoptable segment**, the barriers between groups, and what must change for adoption to continue. A product that works for technical enthusiasts may require a completely different support system to sell to conservative enterprises. A course appealing to highly motivated early adopters may fail for the mainstream learner without different accountability and prerequisites.

## 57. Timing means the alignment of several clocks

The earliest possible date is rarely the best possible entry date. A market becomes commercially viable when several conditions align:

**Technology readiness:** does the product work adequately at acceptable cost?  
**Customer readiness:** do buyers recognize the job and have the skills, trust and willingness to change?  
**Complementary infrastructure:** do distribution, payments, devices, data, suppliers and integrations exist?  
**Institutional readiness:** do standards, regulations, budgets and procurement pathways permit adoption?  
**Founder readiness:** can this team credibly deliver, finance and acquire the product now?

Different clocks can disagree. The technology may be ready while buyers are not. Buyers may be eager while infrastructure is absent. A founder can be technically brilliant but lack the sales access required by the opportunity. Timing analysis is the study of these mismatches.

## 58. The first-mover advantage is conditional

Early entry can help when the firm acquires scarce sites, channels, customer learning, standards, network density, or reputation ahead of rivals. But early entry can also impose research expense, category-education cost, immature infrastructure and uncertain demand. Followers may learn from the pioneer's mistakes or use improved technology. The classic Lieberman–Montgomery analysis considers both first-mover advantages and disadvantages rather than a universal law [R5].

The useful question is not *Can we be first?* but *What asset becomes inaccessible or much more expensive after someone else enters, and can we accumulate it while creating value?* If the answer is “nothing,” moving first may be irrelevant. If the first mover must spend years teaching customers to want a product a later entrant can cheaply copy, early entry can be a disadvantage.

## 59. A new market often carries a category-creation tax

When buyers do not recognize a category, the founder must teach them the problem, explain why existing solutions are inadequate, establish credible proof, create a budget classification, and make purchasing legitimate. Each step consumes attention and money.

The better entry tactic is often to anchor the new capability in an **old, painful, budgeted job**. Instead of pitching “the future of autonomous agents,” a startup might propose a bounded, auditable reduction in a specific administrative backlog—provided it can deliver it. Instead of “the university of the AI era,” a new school might offer rigorously assessed capability for a defined professional task.

Category creation can yield large eventual upside, but founders should not treat the absence of competitors as the absence of alternatives. **Doing nothing remains a competitor in every new market.**

## 60. Why crises and technological transitions create openings

Large transitions rearrange relative costs and make some capabilities scarce. Remote work changed distribution and collaboration needs. Cloud computing lowered infrastructure capital requirements. AI changes which components of knowledge work are inexpensive to produce and which require validation, access or judgment. Regulatory shifts can create new compliance obligations.

Transitions can be fertile for founders because incumbents are optimized for the old equilibrium. But narratives about “a once-in-a-generation disruption” are cheap. Ask what measurable behavior is already changing: new budgets, job responsibilities, procurement criteria, codebases, workarounds, customer complaints, or willingness to pay. Study the **rate at which change moves into ordinary budgets**, not just conference presentations.

## 61. The window of opportunity can close without the market shrinking

A market can grow and simultaneously become harder to enter. Incumbents secure distribution, buyers standardize procurement, customer acquisition auctions intensify, interoperability standards consolidate, and successful competitors accumulate references. The total industry may be larger while the smallest entrant's path becomes narrower.

Conversely, an incumbent may become vulnerable even in a mature market if its operating model cannot profitably serve a new use case. A constrained niche can open while the mainstream remains stable. Therefore timing is about **relative entry conditions**, not only the growth curve.

## 62. Timing decisions should be expressed as conditional bets

The useful choice is rarely simply “now” or “never.” It may be: enter now with a small service to learn; wait for regulation to clarify before committing capital; publish research to build a reputation before selling; partner with an incumbent to acquire access; or abandon the opportunity if a key complementary technology remains too expensive.

Write the timing thesis in a form that reality can contradict:

> We should test this market during **[period]** because **[specific change]** has made **[job]** more urgent or cheaper to serve. We expect to observe **[buyer behavior]** and **[economics]**. We should defer or revise if **[specific assumption]** fails.

If timing cannot be connected to an observable change, the founder may simply be experiencing enthusiasm.

# Part VI — Competitive Mapping, Market Gaps, and Opportunity Discovery

## 63. Competitor research should tell you what customers cannot get—not how many logos exist

It is easy to make a table of twenty competing companies and their features. It is harder, and much more useful, to identify **which purchasing situations their models are optimized for**, where customers are disappointed, and why those disappointments persist. A market gap is not an empty feature cell. It is an inadequately served, economically important job under the alternatives currently available.

Start by identifying the customer's real shortlist and alternatives, then reconstruct each offering's economics and operating logic. Observe who the provider serves best, what it excludes, the evidence supporting its promise, and why changing its model may be difficult. Customer complaints offer clues but are not proof: a complaint may reflect unrealistic expectations, a rare edge case, or a problem customers will not pay to fix.

## 64. Map four competitive circles

For a specialized AI training offer, consider four groups:

1. **Direct equivalents:** other comparable paid, instructor-led applied programs.
2. **Category neighbors:** universities, MOOCs, technical bootcamps, coaching, certification providers and company academies.
3. **Functional substitutes:** internal mentors, projects, open source, specialist consultants, AI tutors and self-study.
4. **Non-consumption and postponement:** doing nothing, tolerating low capability, or waiting for employer support.

The same method generalizes. A restaurant competes not only with restaurants but with home cooking and delivery. A corporate analytics consultancy competes with internal analysts and software. A new medical device competes with established procedures and avoidance of intervention. The further circles often constrain the product more severely than obvious rival brands.

## 65. Map strategic groups rather than ranking every competitor on one scale

Within an industry, firms often cluster by business model: premium bespoke service, standardized low-price provider, regulated credential, community platform, general-purpose software, vertical specialist, local relationship business, enterprise integrator. These groups make different operating trade-offs.

A meaningful map might use **buyer urgency** on one axis and **degree of individualized expert intervention** on another, or **buyer type** and **basis of trust**. A weak map uses dimensions selected merely to place the founder's imaginary product in an empty quadrant. A good set of axes explains actual purchasing differences and supply economics.

**Empty space on a chart is not automatically a market gap.** It may be empty because customers don't value the combination, distribution is prohibitively expensive, or the operational requirements are mutually contradictory.

## 66. The eight recurring sources of market gaps

Gaps commonly arise from different mechanisms. Each demands a different test.

| Gap mechanism | What customers experience | Why incumbents may leave it open | First test |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Underserved segment** | Important needs buried inside mass offering | Unit economics optimized for average user | Buyer interviews and narrow paid offer |
| **Overserved segment** | Paying for complexity or quality they do not need | Incumbent revenue depends on richer product | Simpler lower-cost complete solution |
| **Access gap** | Good solutions exist but cannot be bought conveniently | Distribution, language, geography, approvals | Channel or locality pilot |
| **Trust gap** | Quality is hard to judge; poor suppliers dominate perceptions | Weak evidence standards | Inspectable demonstration and paid trial |
| **Workflow gap** | Customers must assemble many tools and people | Suppliers own individual components | Bounded end-to-end integration |
| **Cost-structure gap** | Existing solution too expensive to deliver or buy | Legacy systems, specialist labor or assets | New production method with full-cost audit |
| **Timing / transition gap** | New task appears before providers adapt | Incentive conflict, slow learning cycles | Trigger-led paid diagnostic |
| **Incentive / payer gap** | Beneficiary and payer are misaligned | Budget ownership or externalities | Contract with actual beneficiary of savings |

Notice how often the apparent product opportunity is really a **transaction, distribution, trust or business-model problem**.

## 67. The overserved customer may be easier to win than the underserved customer

An underserved buyer needs more capability, reliability, assurance or attention than mainstream offers provide. Serving them better may be expensive. An overserved buyer needs less complexity and may gladly switch to a simpler, cheaper solution with lower support requirements.

Christensen's disruption framework describes processes in which simpler and more affordable products initially serve overlooked customers or nonconsumers and may later improve and move upmarket [R6]. It is a specific theory of competitive dynamics, not a synonym for every clever startup or technological breakthrough. For a founder, its practical use is to examine **where incumbents' economics make them unwilling to compete**, especially when a new cost structure makes a simple offer sustainable.

## 68. Look for ugly workarounds, not only explicit complaints

Customers often normalize inconvenience. A finance team copies data manually every month. An engineer asks coworkers to review AI outputs informally. A faculty member repeatedly rebuilds practical lessons for a syllabus disconnected from current tools. A shop owner stocks extra inventory because delivery is unreliable.

A workaround can reveal demand if it costs significant resources and persists. But it also needs an alternative explanation: perhaps the workaround is good enough; perhaps a formal tool is not worth the implementation burden; perhaps there is no budget owner. **The presence of a workaround makes an opportunity worth investigating, not automatically worth entering.**

An especially revealing question is: *What improvised arrangement would stop functioning if the most experienced person in this organization left?* Such arrangements can expose recurring, underpriced specialist needs.

## 69. Negative space in the customer journey

Map a customer's activity before, during, and after using existing products. Many gaps appear **between** products rather than inside them:

- Before purchase: customers cannot diagnose what they need.
- During setup: buyers cannot integrate the product with existing systems.
- During use: expert feedback, reliability or coordination is missing.
- At decision time: proof and assurance are insufficient.
- After use: results cannot be measured or transferred into real work.

A technical institute may find that lecture content is abundant while reliable diagnosis, sustained practice, assessed independence and employer-legible evidence remain difficult. This is a plausible source of value, but the buyer must actually care enough to pay for it.

## 70. Competitive price maps need matched offers

When comparing courses, do not place a free playlist, a university degree, a 20-hour workshop, an individual tutoring package and an enterprise team engagement into one price ranking as though each were the same product. Normalize where possible by **outcome, learner readiness, duration, review intensity, assurance, credential status, eligibility, buyer, and total effort**.

For software compare implemented workflows, seats or usage, security and maintenance. For FMCG compare comparable units, usage occasions, channel and repeat economics. For consulting compare scope, senior attention, deliverables and assumed client inputs.

Also distinguish advertised from realized price. A provider advertising “₹1 lakh course” may routinely discount; another advertising a low base price may charge separately for necessary mentoring. Public information is useful but incomplete. Record the source and confidence for every comparison.

## 71. Diagnose the incumbent's underlying business model

A competitor's product is easier to understand if you ask how it makes money. Does it rely on large cohorts and low human attention? Revenue-sharing university partnerships? A famous instructor and expensive acquisition? Cheap prerecorded content and high conversion? Enterprise retainers? Cross-selling software? Low unit margin and huge distribution density?

Each model enables some promises and discourages others. A high-volume course provider may have difficulty offering extensive individual review without changing price or margins. A bespoke consultancy may resist productizing a repeatable diagnostic if it cannibalizes high-priced engagements. A supermarket incumbent may be disinclined to serve a tiny local delivery occasion.

But incumbent cost structures are rarely public, and speculative stories can become dangerous. Distinguish *observable operational facts* from *guesses about incentives*. Test whether the opening persists when the competitor is assumed to be competent and financially capable.

## 72. Geographic, demographic, and behavioral gaps are not equivalent

A geographic niche can work when physical access, regulation, local relationships or travel costs matter. A demographic niche can help when language, spending power, accessibility or identity shape demand. A behavioral or situational niche can be stronger when the purchasing trigger and job determine choice.

A founder may start in one Indian city to build trusted industry partnerships; another may sell a tightly defined remote engineering service internationally from day one. Neither geography is intrinsically superior. Choose the axis that makes **customers reachable, demand concentrated, and operations repeatable**.

## 73. Build a live market map that records uncertainty

A market map should be a living research instrument, not a decorative diagram. For each candidate customer–job–transaction combination, record:

| Field | What to capture |
|---|---|
| Customer and trigger | Actual buyer type; what happened before purchase |
| Job and outcome | Measurable progress; current workaround |
| Budget and spending | Evidence of approved budgets and past purchases |
| Alternatives | Direct vendors, substitutes, in-house work, non-consumption |
| Competitive structure | Suppliers, buyer power, rivalry, entry and substitutes |
| Value-chain position | Where the offering participates and who captures margin |
| Access | Names of viable channels and approval paths |
| Economics | Price, acquisition, delivery, cash cycle, capacity |
| Dynamics | Growth driver, timing, technological or policy shifts |
| Fit | What the founder brings and lacks |
| Evidence strength | Observation, verified external data, report, inference, assumption |
| Next decisive test | Smallest action that changes the go/no-go decision |

This document is the output of the founder's investigation. Its purpose is to prevent vague admiration for a sector from taking the place of actual commercial knowledge.

## 74. The red-team competitor memo

For the strongest candidate market, write a memo from the perspective of the best incumbent:

> “This entrant is trying to win **[customers]** because our model does not currently serve **[job]** well. We could respond by **[copying, discounting, partnering, buying or bypassing]**. Our strongest assets are **[capabilities and access]**. We would choose **[response]** because the economics are **[reason]**.”

Now write the entrant's reply. If the answer relies on incumbents being stupid, inattentive or culturally incapable without evidence, the opportunity thesis is weak. If the entrant can explain a genuine customer mismatch and a cost or incentive reason why the incumbent's rational response is constrained, the opening becomes more interesting.

# Part VII — How Market Selection Changes Across Industries

## 75. There is one economic logic but no universal entry tactic

A founder selling an industrial valve, a premium biscuit, AI software, specialized consulting, and a university program does not confront the same market-entry problem. What matters differs in purchase frequency, quality observability, capital, buyer concentration, distribution, regulation, and uncertainty. It is a mistake to transplant a tactic from one sector without understanding the mechanism that made it work there.

The following atlas is a **diagnostic starting point**, not an industry ranking. Each sector contains very different submarkets.

| Setting | Main demand unit | Structural bottleneck often worth investigating | What to verify before entry |
|---|---|---|---|
| FMCG staples | Purchase occasion and repeat household / shopper | Retail access, habit, product consistency | Sell-through, repeat, trade margin, wastage |
| Premium consumer goods | Specific use occasion and identity/quality preference | Brand legitimacy, design, provenance, channel | Full-price repeat or recommendation, inventory economics |
| Commodity manufacturing | Specified grade and delivered quantity | Delivered cost, reliable supply, capital utilization | Landed cost through a full cycle and buyer concentration |
| Consumer hardware | Use case and replacement cycle | Manufacturing, service, distribution, failure rate | Warranty economics, working capital, returns |
| Horizontal SaaS | Account/workspace and recurring job | Distribution, activation, integration | Paid retention, CAC, support, switching |
| Vertical SaaS | Consequential industry workflow | Domain integration, proof, procurement | Paid workflow use, security, deployment effort |
| AI applications | Successful task / workflow | Evaluation, data access, trust, distribution | Performance after model changes, cost per success |
| Marketplace | Completed match or transaction | Local/category liquidity and trust | Repeat matching, multihoming, take rate |
| Boutique consulting | Problem episode or relationship | Expert access, trust, scope, client implementation | Fee, client acquisition, delivery hours, references |
| Enterprise L&D | Team capability and budgeted intervention | Budget owner, applied impact, procurement | Sponsor, business metric, repeatable delivery |
| Independent education | Learner transformation and tuition | Buyer trust, effort, outcomes, expert time | Paid commitment, assessed improvement, attrition |
| College partnerships | Institution's academic requirement and contract | Approvals, timetable, faculty coordination | Paid pilot, collection, repeat procurement |
| Healthcare | Patient need and treatment episode | Safety, licenses, clinician availability, trust | Outcomes, insurance/payment, legal feasibility |
| Biotech / deep tech | Validated scientific or industrial application | Research, regulatory approval, capital | Milestone de-risking, route to adoption, funding |
| Financial services | Account, payment, loan or insured risk | Trust, licensing, capital, underwriting | Risk-adjusted unit economics and compliance |
| Infrastructure | Capacity or regulated service delivered | Permits, sites, financing, network density | Long-run cost, utilization, contracts, return on capital |
| Local services | Job performed within a catchment | Geographic availability, reliability, referrals | Local density, repeat, labor and occupancy costs |

## 76. FMCG: a large population does not guarantee a viable shelf business

Consider a new packaged food product. The founder observes that almost everyone eats. That is a very large need space and a useless estimate of their market. A real opportunity requires a particular occasion—breakfast, a commuting snack, a regional taste, a gifting ritual, a dietary requirement supported by truthful claims—and a route to repeated purchase.

The market structure may be monopolistically competitive at consumer level but highly concentrated at distribution or modern retail. Retailers can demand margin, placement fees or promotional support. The product must remain fresh, in stock, and financially viable after manufacturing, returns, expiry, breakage, logistics, marketing, and trade terms. A successful shipment to a distributor is **sell-in**, not proof of consumer **sell-through**.

The entry experiment might focus on one dense micro-territory and measure blind preference where appropriate, first purchase, second and third purchase, retailer replenishment, spoilage, actual margin and repeat availability. An extremely large TAM matters far less at this stage than the economics of a hundred actual transactions and the cost of reproducing them.

## 77. Commodities and industrial inputs: do not invent differentiation where delivered cost rules

For standardized grades of metals, chemicals, fasteners, agricultural products and industrial components, buyers may compare precise specifications and delivery terms. The founder's opportunity may be superior procurement, logistics, quality consistency, inventory finance, batch traceability, reliable supply, or localized availability rather than an unprecedented product.

A serious feasibility study models **through-cycle economics**: material costs, energy, freight, losses, plant utilization, credit risk, inventory carrying cost, working capital, maintenance, compliance and price cycles. A favorable spot-price boom can disguise an unattractive market. The test of a commodity entrant is whether it can sustain lower total delivered cost or create a transaction advantage the buyer will pay for.

## 78. Software and SaaS: usage growth can conceal weak economics

A software market is not always global simply because a product can be accessed over the internet. Language, security requirements, data-residency rules, integrations, budget holders, procurement, and compatibility can segment it sharply. The founder must distinguish free users, trial activation, sustained usage, paid conversion, gross retention, account expansion, cloud costs and support.

Horizontal software can seek broad demand but face powerful incumbents and acquisition auctions. Vertical software can address expensive specialized workflows and earn strong relevance, yet have smaller account counts and greater implementation cost. AI applications add model-provider dependence, variable inference costs and the risk that the underlying model makes a feature obsolete or enables the platform to bundle it.

A reasonable first market is often a narrow consequential workflow with an identifiable sponsor and inspectable result. Its initial TAM may look modest. Its long-run opportunity may expand through adjacent workflows if the same distribution, data rights, and product architecture transfer economically. Do not assume that all adjacent revenue is available simply because a roadmap contains the features.

## 79. Marketplaces: an atomic market is more useful than a national market

A marketplace for skilled instructors, contractors, healthcare appointments, used equipment, or specialized suppliers must solve matching and trust on both sides. The relevant market size is affected by transaction density, frequency, take rate, disintermediation and fulfillment.

If buyers cannot find a credible supplier at the moment of need, thousands of distant supplier listings may be worthless. If sellers can easily transact outside the platform after being introduced, a large gross merchandise value may produce little retained fee. Enter through one **atomic market**—a customer group, geography, category or time window with enough repeated successful matches to create real utility. Measure match completion, quality, time-to-match, repeat, take rate after incentives, and concentration. Expand only where added participants improve rather than dilute matching.

## 80. Consulting and professional services: an apparent market can be founder labor in disguise

Advisory and specialist services can be excellent first businesses because they require little initial infrastructure, reveal customer problems directly, and may generate cash quickly. They can also produce a ceiling when all credibility, selling, and delivery depend on the founder.

A particularly attractive niche has recognizable costly problems, identifiable budget owners, repeatable diagnosis, high trust needs, credible founder expertise, and the potential to build reusable methods or a delivery team. A weak niche requires perpetual free proposals, highly customized work, delayed collections, and a different skill set for every project.

Track **realized fee per engagement, paid discovery versus free consulting, sales cycle, fully loaded delivery hours, change requests, concentration, collection, and repeat contracts**. Do not call a lucrative project a scalable market until you can explain how the next ten projects will be acquired and delivered without ten times the founder's effort.

## 81. Capital-heavy deep tech: test different uncertainties in the correct sequence

An advanced materials, semiconductor, medical-device or biotech startup may have a large downstream market but face sequential scientific, manufacturing, regulatory, financing and commercialization risks. The correct early experiment is often not a landing-page conversion test. It may be a technical feasibility study, a validated prototype, an independent lab result, a regulatory consultation or a purchase-intent agreement contingent on performance.

Market selection must include the **capital required before evidence**, intellectual property rights, manufacturing partners, standards, decision timelines, distribution, and who pays for clinical or industrial validation. Large markets can be real yet impossible to approach with the founder's current resources. Partnerships, grants, licenses and venture financing can alter feasibility, but they also alter ownership and governance. Do not mistake an impressive scientific achievement for a signed purchasing pathway.

## 82. Local businesses: the relevant market is often bounded by travel time and habit

A neighborhood clinic, repair shop, bakery or tutoring center may compete primarily within a local catchment. Proximity, reliability, trust, service hours and local repeat purchase matter more than national share. Market sizing should use realistic travel times, customer occasion frequency, local incomes, traffic patterns, competition, occupancy cost and staffing.

A local niche may be perfectly good for sustained owner income and poorly suited to venture financing. The market-selection decision should reflect what the founder actually wants: geographic replication, one excellent site, a franchise model, or a long-term community institution. Those are different ambitions and operating systems.

## 83. Education: three structurally different markets share the same classroom language

A **direct learner market** sells personal progress under money and time constraints. The customer must believe that the educational experience will be worth the sacrifice. Quality is difficult to evaluate and outcomes partly depend on the learner.

An **institutional education-services market** sells to universities, colleges or education providers that have their own academic standards, governance, calendars, procurement processes and reputational risks. A successful product must fit into their operating model, not merely teach well in isolation.

An **enterprise capability market** sells changed employee performance or organizational capability to an economic sponsor. The relevant alternatives include consulting, internal training, hiring, software and process redesign. Enterprise clients may pay much more per contract but demand customization, confidentiality, measurement and procurement discipline.

The same technical knowledge may be useful in all three, yet the differences in buying process, economics and service responsibilities are large enough to warrant **three separate market analyses**. A founder who calls them “three channels for one course” may understate their strategic and operational complexity.

## 84. Education's peculiar demand: the buyer is purchasing an uncertain future

A learner cannot fully inspect the most important outcome before purchasing education. Even afterward, it may be hard to identify the institution's causal contribution relative to prior ability, motivation, peer influence and external opportunities. This informational asymmetry can favor established credentials and reputation, but it also opens a gap for rigorous, transparent demonstrations of competence.

For a new independent provider, the market-selection question is whether enough buyers value **learning mechanisms the founder can actually demonstrate**—diagnosis, accountable practice, feedback and verified capability—without requiring prestige or employment guarantees the institution does not yet possess.

The market is not defined merely by the curriculum topic. It is defined by the learner's starting capability, desired transformation, urgency, funding, credible alternatives, available weekly hours and the evidence required before payment. These variables can change the market more than the difference between a “machine learning” and “generative AI” syllabus.

## 85. Education's regulatory and signaling distinction

Private professional training and recognized degree-awarding higher education are not interchangeable institutional products. A founder may aspire to build a research university while currently selling non-degree technical learning. Market sizing, competitive positioning, academic claims, licenses and financing must correspond to the **actual lawful product** at each stage. A regulated credential market may have high entry barriers that cannot be crossed through branding.

An institute can earn trust through teaching and assessment before it gains any formal credential power. But those achievements do not entitle it to promise university-equivalent recognition. The attractive initial market must exist on the educational value it can presently provide.

# Part VIII — A Rigorous Opportunity-Selection Method

## 86. Opportunity selection is a constrained portfolio decision

A first-time founder usually has more plausible ideas than time to test them. The task is not to find the objectively best industry in the world. It is to choose **the next opportunity for which the founder has a credible route to evidence, customers, value creation and sustainable economics**, subject to strategic boundaries and risk.

A useful conceptual model is:

\[
\text{Founding attractiveness} = f(\text{customer demand},\text{economic structure},\text{competitive entry},\text{founder fit},\text{access},\text{capital/time},\text{long-run options}).
\]

This is intentionally *not* a formula that can be computed by averaging seven survey scores. Factors interact, some are deal breakers, and the information is poor. A market with strong demand but impossible regulatory access is not rescued by a high average score. A founder who can reach ten paying customers in a modest market may rationally outperform one pursuing an enormous but inaccessible category.

## 87. First apply non-negotiable gates

Before comparative ranking, exclude or defer opportunities that violate essential requirements. An illustrative gate set:

**Legal and ethical gate:** Can the business operate honestly, with required permissions, without harmful or misleading claims?  
**Problem gate:** Is there evidence of a meaningful job rather than generalized admiration?  
**Purchase gate:** Is there a plausible economic buyer and transaction mechanism?  
**Founder capability gate:** Can the founder deliver a safe, credible first version or access the required expertise?  
**Survival gate:** Can a decisive test be financed and delivered without risking ruin or breaking other commitments?  
**Mission-boundary gate:** Would doing this fundamentally contradict the organization's declared principles or strategic limits?

A failure may mean **defer**, not **never**. A future university program might fail the current authorization and capital gates but remain consistent with the long-range mission. A highly regulated fintech service might become feasible after obtaining partners and licenses. The gate prevents premature commitment, not imagination.

## 88. Compare opportunities on separate axes, not a single mystical score

After passing gates, compare opportunities across **customer pull, economic quality, founder access and competence, speed of learning, capital efficiency, repeatability, strategic accumulation, and downside**.

| Dimension | Evidence that matters | A seductive but weak proxy |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer urgency | Recent purchase triggers, budget and costly workarounds | General interest in the subject |
| Competitive opening | Specific dissatisfaction plus incumbent trade-off | Few competitors listed online |
| Value capture | Realistic prices, contribution and bargaining structure | TAM or category revenue |
| Reachability | Identifiable qualified buyers and actionable channels | Total social-media audience |
| Founder fit | Relevant demonstrated skill and sales access | Prestige of previous employers alone |
| Time to first evidence | Feasible paid pilot and defined decision point | Fast website launch |
| Economic repeatability | Comparable transactions and bounded delivery burden | One favorable contract |
| Optionality | Reusable skills, relationships, data rights, systems | A grand adjacency narrative |
| Downside | Cash exposure, fixed liabilities, reputational risk | Expected upside alone |

Treat ordinal ratings as prompts for discussion, not measurements. Record confidence separately. In particular, **don't reward an idea for being highly uncertain merely because its imagined upside is enormous**.

## 89. Opportunity cost is often the binding resource

Choosing one market consumes time, attention, money and identity that cannot be invested elsewhere. For a founder retaining a full-time job, 15–25 available hours per week are scarce enough that two promising experiments may be mutually exclusive. A ten-hour weekly commitment to a custom enterprise project may displace interviews for the core educational product or preparation of a teaching pilot.

The opportunity cost of a project is not just the foregone salary or cash expenditure. It includes **the best alternative use of the founder's restricted attention** and the learning or durable assets that alternative could produce. This is why a low-margin paid pilot that exposes a decisive assumption may be strategically better than months of vague planning—and why a large, completely bespoke consulting contract can be strategically expensive even when it produces cash.

## 90. Estimate expected value, but do not fake probabilities

If you can specify bounded outcomes and credible probabilities, expected-value analysis can compare experiments. More commonly at the earliest stage, founders lack calibrated probability estimates. Replacing that uncertainty with arbitrary 70% and 80% “probabilities” does not make the model rigorous.

Use three complementary views:

**Economic scenarios:** what is the downside, base outcome and plausible high outcome after full costs and elapsed time?  
**Evidence-to-action rules:** what result would change which decision?  
**Option value:** how does a small experiment preserve the right to invest later without committing the entire resource base now?

Value of information matters most when a discovery result would actually change action. A hundred interviews that never alter a decision are not intrinsically valuable. One paid pilot that disproves a central assumption may save the founder from building an expensive product nobody wants.

## 91. The two-stage selection problem

A founder should not compare fully built hypothetical businesses as though each already exists. Instead compare **the first experiments that might reveal whether the businesses deserve to exist**.

Stage one chooses which opportunities are **worth investigating**. Stage two selects the business only when evidence is adequate to justify further commitment. This is an important distinction. At the beginning the correct decision may be: *Run one D2C demand test and one small institutional pilot in sequence, then compare what we learned.* It need not be: *Declare D2C the only possible future of the company.*

Choosing experiments rather than premature permanent identity preserves flexibility, provided the experiments do not scatter attention across too many unrelated jobs.

## 92. The shortest path to information is not always the shortest path to a sale

A founder can obtain enthusiastic reactions to a course idea in a day, but may need weeks to test real payment and months to measure long-run learning. Enterprise contracts may take a quarter to approve even when the problem is acute. FMCG may show trial quickly but needs repeat purchase to prove preference. A scientific product may need a lab result before a buyer can evaluate it at all.

Choose **decision-relevant evidence appropriate to the market's clock**. Do not demand a one-week sales experiment from a regulated hospital or treat a paid webinar as proof of six-month educational transformation. Evidence timing is part of feasibility.

## 93. A research sequence for a first-time founder

A strong investigation can proceed as follows. The exact number of conversations depends on heterogeneity, access and decision stakes; the counts are operating suggestions, not guaranteed statistical sample sizes.

**Phase A — Frame.** Define five to ten candidate buyer–job–transaction opportunities, not merely five technologies. Write what each buyer would do without you. Apply ethical, legal, resource and mission gates. Remove obvious impossibilities.

**Phase B — Desk research.** For the remaining ideas, collect government statistics, industry studies, public filings, product and price comparisons, job postings, current alternatives, procurement requirements, platform rules, and the best available demand indicators. Mark source dates and boundaries. Do not confuse reports with demand validation.

**Phase C — Field discovery.** Interview recent buyers, nonbuyers, budget owners, user representatives and relevant sellers. Observe how the job is done. Reconstruct real recent decisions, not hypothetical purchases. A few interviews may reveal a crucial mismatch; dozens can still mislead if sampled from a convenient but unrepresentative group.

**Phase D — Economics.** Construct a simple unit model: price, conversion, channel cost, labor, capacity, refunds, working capital, collections and normalized founder time. Make a competitive-response assumption explicit.

**Phase E — Behavioral proof.** Offer a proportionate, truthful, complete first purchase or pilot. Measure what buyers actually sacrifice and what delivery actually costs. Where sale cycles are long, use intermediate costly commitments appropriate to the category without calling them revenue.

**Phase F — Decide.** Continue, narrow, postpone, change payer, move to another value-chain layer, or reject. Write why. Update the map. Preserve the alternative opportunities without treating all of them as simultaneous businesses.

## 94. Sources: use a research triangulation ladder

A founder should seek three different evidence streams and notice when they conflict:

**Administrative and financial evidence:** public market statistics, company filings, procurement notices, budgets, prices actually charged where observable, regulatory documentation, payroll and transaction data available lawfully. Useful for counts and economics, but definitions may differ.

**Behavioral evidence:** interviews about past decisions, observation of workarounds, actual payment, applications, pilots, usage and renewals. Useful for mechanisms but often selected and small.

**Mechanism evidence:** an explicit causal account of why a buyer would change behavior, why an incumbent has not served the need, how the business earns margin and what may happen if conditions change.

No single stream is enough. A recent industry report can identify a broad trend without proving a niche purchase. Ten paid customers can validate a local proposition without proving a nationwide TAM. A compelling strategic theory can be entirely wrong if buyers behave differently.

## 95. The opportunity evidence ledger

Build a table with one row per decisive claim:

| Claim | Current evidence | Confidence | What would falsify it | Cheapest meaningful test |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| “Working engineers pay for applied critique” | Some conversations, prior comparable purchases | Preliminary | Appropriate prospects decline real paid review | Offer a bounded paid review clinic |
| “Colleges will purchase curriculum-integrated AI modules” | Meetings and positive reactions | Unvalidated | No budget, approvals or signed paid pilot | Present a scoped priced proposal |
| “Enterprise buyers sponsor workflow-tied training” | Practitioner discussions | Unvalidated | Managers prefer internal alternatives or cannot obtain approval | Offer a diagnostic with budget owner |
| “Reviews can be delivered within founder capacity” | Hours model, not actual pilot | Assumption | Review time and support exceed budget | Time an end-to-end trial |

This simple distinction between **fact, report, inference and speculation** can prevent months of false confidence.

## 96. How to avoid the false precision of weighted scoring

Weighted scoring is helpful for organizing conversation but dangerous when a founder treats a sum of ordinal ratings as a probability of success. Consider an opportunity rated 5/5 on market size, 5/5 on trend, 1/5 on reachable buyers, 1/5 on ability to deliver. An average of 3 could conceal the fact that it is commercially impossible now.

Use a **non-compensatory first screen**: if purchase feasibility, legality, founder capacity or an acceptable downside is absent, do not proceed to commitment. After that, compare remaining ideas by dominance: is one clearly better on all critical dimensions? Where they are close, choose the experiment with the highest expected decision value relative to money and time spent. Only then use a weighted score, if useful, as a transparent record of priorities—not an oracle.

## 97. Stop conditions matter more than inspirational milestones

Define in advance what would make you stop or narrow the market thesis. Examples:

- A large share of the apparent “demand” comes from people unwilling or unable to pay.
- Purchasing authority sits with an inaccessible stakeholder.
- Buyers already have a satisfactory low-cost substitute and show no costly dissatisfaction.
- The first successful sale requires unrepeatable founder favors.
- Delivered quality depends on a level of human labor that the target segment will not finance.
- Customers cannot commit enough time to realize the promised benefit.
- Regulation or data rights prevent economically feasible delivery.
- Distribution costs at realistic scale exceed retained contribution.
- A technology change removes the job faster than the firm can adapt.

A failed market hypothesis is an investment in knowledge only if the founder was prepared to recognize failure. Otherwise research becomes a way to defend the original idea.

## 98. The market-selection decision memo

A disciplined founder can conclude a market investigation in two pages:

> **The buyer and job.** Who is the precise decision-maker, in what situation, and what progress matters?  
> **Current alternative.** What do they pay or do now, and what remains unsatisfactory?  
> **Demand evidence.** What recent real behavior, not admiration, indicates priority?  
> **Market boundaries and size.** Which customers and spending are actually in scope?  
> **Structure and profit pool.** Where do buyer, supplier, substitute and competitor pressures lie?  
> **Entry route.** How will the first ten buyers discover, trust and pay us?  
> **Economics and capacity.** What do we retain after honest full costs?  
> **Founder fit.** Why can we learn and deliver unusually well here?  
> **Timing and rivals.** Why now, and what response should we expect?  
> **Downside and option.** What is the most we risk before decisive evidence?  
> **Recommendation.** Test, pursue, defer, narrow, or reject—and what would change the decision?

A market that cannot be described this way has probably not been understood deeply enough to justify a large commitment.

# Part IX — Bayes Institute: A Real Opportunity-Selection Problem

## 99. Begin with the starting position, not the desired university

Bayes Institute's enduring aspiration is to help human possibility become human capability, with a long-term emphasis on STEM, rigorous learning and eventually research. That aspiration is a valuable direction; it is not evidence that any particular market will finance it. The institution is at an extremely early stage. The founder has substantial experience in AI engineering, software, statistics, technical instruction and curriculum design, and has explored several possible commercial routes. But **Bayes itself has not yet earned a repeatable institutional sales engine, graduate-outcome reputation, university-level recognition, or a mature employer network**. Any market analysis that assumes those assets exist is invalid.

Its near-term constraints are concrete: limited starting cash, a sustainable part-time founder commitment alongside full-time professional employment, no large campus, limited faculty capacity, a preference for asset-light operation, and unwillingness to rely on placement guarantees, predatory student financing or early equity dilution. The first market must be feasible within those boundaries. Past work experience may demonstrate founder competence but does not imply endorsement by any current or former employer.

The question is not “Which version of the eventual research university should be built first?” It is **“Which currently reachable buyers will pay Bayes for a real and verifiable contribution, in a way that produces cash and capabilities rather than exhausting the founder?”**

## 100. The macro market is real; the Bayes transaction is unproved

India has a large higher-education system. The Ministry of Education's July 2026 release of the 2022–23 and 2023–24 All India Survey on Higher Education reports about **4.50 crore** students enrolled in higher education in 2023–24, including about **1.02 crore** STEM enrollments [R7]. These figures provide educational context. They are **not** a TAM for Bayes: a university student is not necessarily a self-paying buyer of advanced non-degree AI instruction, and public enrollment data do not reveal college outsourcing budgets or enterprise learning expenditure. The survey is based on self-reported institutional data and has stated limitations.

A 2024 NASSCOM AI-skilling report discussed growth expectations for India's AI software and services market and the need for a larger AI talent pool [R8]. That report is useful as a dated statement about industry expectations, not as proof that a specific 2026 learner will pay for a specific course. Its projections can miss, and a growing AI labor market may generate free employer-provided learning as well as paid external demand.

A serious founder should welcome this tension. **Macro tailwinds can explain why an opportunity deserves investigation; micro transactions determine whether a company deserves investment.**

## 101. Bayes does not face one market, but a choice among distinct customer systems

An initial map contains at least the following candidate opportunity clusters:

| Opportunity cluster | Economic buyer | What they might purchase | What Bayes must prove |
|---|---|---|---|
| Working software developers | Individual professional; sometimes employer | Practical AI engineering transformation | Pain, protected learning time, payment, improvement |
| Quantitative analysts and technical finance professionals | Individual or sponsor | Applied AI and modeling capability | Clear job-relevant outcome and proper prerequisites |
| Technical college department | College leadership / authorized department | Industry-informed elective, assessed lab or faculty enablement | Budget, approval, integration and reliable delivery |
| Enterprise engineering or analytics team | Manager, L&D sponsor, business owner | Workflow-specific AI capability diagnostic and training | Business problem, sponsorship, procurement and impact |
| Small company seeking AI implementation | Owner / operating leader | Bounded diagnostic, prototype, evaluation or advisory service | Real operating need, delivery rights, repeatability |
| Long-run university / laboratory / research partner | Academic or research sponsor | Joint projects, funded research, specialized capability | Research quality, governance, legal rights and external validation |

These should not all become products. They are an **idea-space map** that needs disciplined narrowing. They differ in whom Bayes must persuade, how much evidence is required, payment and collection timing, customer concentration, operational burden and capability accumulation.

## 102. Candidate A: a D2C applied AI engineering cohort

The hypothesis: some employed software engineers need more than public tutorials. They face responsibility for designing, evaluating and deploying AI-enabled systems, and value rigorous human critique, bounded accountability and independent assessment enough to pay for a demanding program.

**Customer job:** Become more competent at a current or upcoming technical responsibility, not merely acquire another certificate.  
**Buyer:** A developer willing and able to invest personal funds and sustained time.  
**Substitutes:** Free resources, AI assistants, internal mentorship, side projects, MOOCs, existing bootcamps and waiting.  
**Likely demand friction:** Establishing the urgency of the gap, credibility of Bayes's added value and the learner's ability to devote real weekly time.  
**Structural risk:** High-quality review consumes expert hours; direct consumer acquisition can be expensive; many nominal competitors exist; students may seek placement signaling that Bayes does not provide.  
**Strategic asset:** Measurable learner work, assessment standards, a feedback system, examples of teaching excellence and eventually earned reputation.

The first task is to **discover which specific engineering responsibility** makes the buyer purchase. “AI will change software engineering” is far too broad. “My team must deploy a production retrieval service next quarter and I cannot evaluate its failures” is much closer to a meaningful buying situation. Whether that situation is frequent and worth a paid educational product is precisely what research must establish.

## 103. Candidate B: a college partnership pilot

The hypothesis: some Indian engineering colleges have a gap between current curricular capacity and the practical instruction, applied projects, faculty support or external assessment they want to provide. Bayes can supply a bounded intervention more economically or reliably than building it entirely in-house.

**Customer job:** Deliver a particular academic learning or capability objective without disrupting the college's schedule, faculty responsibilities, standards, budget or reputation.  
**Buyer:** An authorized academic and financial decision system, not simply a professor who likes the idea.  
**Substitutes:** Internal faculty, existing vendors, university online content, guest sessions, prior training suppliers and doing nothing.  
**Likely demand friction:** Approvals, academic calendars, contracts, administrative coordination, price, procurement and collection.  
**Structural risk:** Long sales cycles; scope creep; large variation in student readiness; founder travel and management burden; procurement price sensitivity; late payment.  
**Strategic asset:** Faculty-enabling methods, institutional references, curricular integration capability, repeatable assessment standards and regional relationships.

The founder has previously conducted qualitative conversations and smaller training work with colleges in parts of southern India. Such experience provides useful access and market hypotheses. **Positive meetings are not signed pilot contracts**, and an enthusiastic department cannot necessarily approve budgets. The decisive research question is whether a *small, precisely scoped, paid course or lab* can be purchased and repeated while protecting educational standards and sufficient margin.

## 104. Candidate C: enterprise AI capability and workflow training

The hypothesis: certain teams are deploying AI into operations faster than they can develop appropriate engineering evaluation, governance and implementation judgment. The buyer may pay to reduce operational mistakes or improve a concrete workflow, rather than purchasing generic AI literacy.

**Customer job:** Equip a defined team to perform an important workflow with measurable improvement.  
**Buyer:** A business or engineering sponsor with a budget; L&D, procurement, legal and security may also matter.  
**Substitutes:** Internal learning, hiring, external consulting, software products, vendor training or leaving the process unchanged.  
**Likely demand friction:** Demonstrating a sufficiently costly problem, finding the true budget owner, vendor approval, proof of delivery, information security and implementation dependencies.  
**Structural risk:** Long procurement, bespoke scope, founder dependence, client data restrictions, payment delays and weak causal attribution.  
**Strategic asset:** Industry-relevant problem knowledge, referenceable results where permitted, reusable evaluations, practitioner relationships and possible applied research connections.

Enterprise engagements can carry higher contract values than individual tuition but are not automatically more profitable. A complex sales process and one highly customized project can absorb months of constrained founder capacity. Nor does client budget automatically imply interest in training: a manager may prefer software, an internal hire, a consultant or a vendor solution. Bayes must learn which **specific service** is an acceptable purchase inside that company's workflow.

## 105. The first opportunity is not necessarily the biggest revenue line

Suppose the founder can win one large enterprise contract but must personally deliver an unfamiliar, confidential, custom software system for three months. The fee could be attractive; the project may nevertheless delay the first educational program, generate little reusable curricular knowledge, and increase employment or delivery conflicts. Another smaller college project might produce modest cash but a useful faculty process and repeatable teaching assets. A D2C studio might be economically small yet prove the institution's core transformation system and produce durable learning evidence.

There is no universal rule that the D2C course should come first, or that B2B funds everything. Compare **cash contribution, founder-hour consumption, downside, repeatability, shared capability accumulation and customer access** separately. The best initial action may be a service that funds learning, but a service that turns Bayes into a permanent bespoke agency could be strategically costly.

## 106. A provisional opportunity comparison—not a verdict

Before paid pilots, the evidence supports **hypotheses**, not rankings with scientific scores:

| Dimension | D2C technical cohort | College applied pilot | Enterprise AI pilot |
|---|---|---|---|
| Founder technical fit | Strong potential | Strong for selected STEM topics | Strong for specific AI workflows |
| Known customer access | Needs fresh prospect discovery | Some prior conversations, incomplete contract evidence | Unverified buyer network and procurement access |
| Buyer willingness to pay | Unvalidated | Unvalidated for new scope | Unvalidated |
| Time to behavioral proof | Potentially short for a paid sprint | May depend on college calendar and approval | May require stakeholder process |
| Delivery burden | Repeated learner feedback and support | Timetabled sections, faculty/student coordination | Potentially intensive customization |
| Main cash risk | Low enrollment, refunds, fixed cohort preparation | Delayed collection and committed delivery | Scope creep, delayed contract and collection |
| Most direct asset for Bayes | Learning-outcome and assessment system | Academic delivery and partnerships | Industry problems and applied capability |
| Primary disconfirming signal | Qualified people will not pay or protect time | No sponsor, budget or workable contract | No budget owner or verifiable business need |

This is not a claim that any route is already profitable, credible or scalable. The founder can now choose **tests that most efficiently resolve the missing evidence**.

## 107. A hypothetical three-route economics exercise

To compare markets, build the economics of a *specified first engagement*, not a grand five-year revenue projection. Consider three completely invented examples:

| Item | D2C six-week studio | College assessed-module pilot | Enterprise AI diagnostic/training |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Illustrative revenue | 10 × ₹30,000 = **₹3,00,000** | **₹3,00,000** contract | **₹4,00,000** contract |
| Cash delivery, tools and support | ₹85,000 | ₹95,000 | ₹1,10,000 |
| Cash sales or transaction spending | ₹25,000 | ₹20,000 | ₹20,000 |
| Founder labor priced notionally at ₹1,000/hr | 110 hrs = ₹1,10,000 | 110 hrs = ₹1,10,000 | 150 hrs = ₹1,50,000 |
| **Illustrative residual** | **₹80,000** | **₹75,000** | **₹1,20,000** |

**Every amount and hour is hypothetical, not Bayes data or a market benchmark.** The residuals are **not net profit**: they omit any unlisted overhead, tax, financing, contingency and other costs; collection delays and defaults can change actual cash, and delivery promises might require far more labor. The founder's notional labor allocation is an analytical cost, not cash necessarily paid to the founder.

The analysis shows why **headline contract value does not decide the market**. The enterprise option has the highest example residual but consumes more founder time and may have a much longer sales and cash cycle. The D2C scenario assumes ten real buyers will pay for an unproven new offer. The college scenario assumes an authorized institution signs and pays. None of those assumptions has been demonstrated.

A founder with a full-time job should also compare opportunity **per constrained hour** and the shape of the obligation. A project consuming 150 hours in four weeks may be infeasible even if 150 hours spread over three months would be manageable. The economy of a market must fit the actual schedule of delivery, not merely a quarterly total.

## 108. What evidence would make one route superior?

For **D2C**, a persuasive first signal is a meaningful number of appropriately qualified strangers paying a transparent fee for a bounded experience, completing serious work, and showing progress on tasks not simply rehearsed in class. An excellent pilot also reveals review minutes, attrition and true acquisition effort.

For **colleges**, the strongest first signal is an authorized, fairly scoped **paid** contract or purchase order with workable payment, evaluation and timetable conditions—followed by delivery and a credible renewal conversation. An MOU or a positive meeting may have value but should not be counted as paid market demand.

For **enterprise**, the strongest first signal is an accountable manager sponsoring a paid, bounded diagnostic or training engagement tied to a specific operational gap, with an agreed baseline, acceptance criteria and data permissions. A manager saying “AI is important” is far weaker.

The correct market may be the one that produces sufficient *verified evidence and repeatable economics* with the least irreversible risk, not the one with the most enthusiastic conversations.

## 109. A 60-to-90-day opportunity-discovery design for Bayes

The calendar below is **illustrative and stage-dependent**, not a promise that enterprise procurement or student transformation will complete in ninety days.

**Days 1–14 — Define and map.** Write three separate opportunity cards: (1) an applied AI capability studio for a narrow class of working engineers, (2) one college lab or elective intervention, (3) one enterprise skills-and-workflow diagnostic. Specify buyer, real job, alternatives, exact first offer, access route, expected obligations, and unit-economics assumptions. Build a small named-account or prospect list using lawful channels and current permission. Do not assume old historical contact lists may be reused.

**Days 15–30 — Discover behavior.** Speak with recent buyers and nonbuyers in each candidate group, emphasizing real recent decisions and workarounds. Ask for the responsible budget or procurement route in B2B. Show one substantive public technical artifact to relevant D2C prospects. Record verbatim objections and what happened when prospects considered a real fee or effort commitment.

**Days 31–45 — Attempt bounded commercial commitments.** Offer a specific paid diagnostic, studio or pilot only where the service is feasible and honestly described. For a college, send a priced scoped proposal to an authorized buyer. For an enterprise, request a funded limited evaluation rather than an open-ended “AI transformation.” For professionals, test a short complete applied experience before assuming demand for a six-month flagship.

**Days 46–70 — Deliver the best supported pilot.** Avoid promising all three in parallel. Prioritize the feasible paid test that actually obtains commitment. Measure buyer value, instructor/reviewer time, quality, cash expenses, support, and the availability of reusable work products. Document limitations.

**Days 71–90 — Update the opportunity portfolio.** Compare real money, actual decision time, obligations, buyer objections, learning outcomes where observed, and reusable assets. Select a leading route for another iteration; keep a secondary route as a *small option* only if the incremental cost is justified. Defer or reject the weakest route based on evidence, not enthusiasm.

A longer program's ultimate effectiveness cannot be established in this period. Nor should a long corporate sales cycle be declared a failure because it did not match a consumer schedule. Align decision milestones with each market's natural clock.

## 110. A constrained founder's research budget

With roughly 15–25 sustainable hours per week available for the venture, the founder should avoid a market-research program that consumes all available time but never asks customers to commit. A possible 20-hour pre-pilot week might allocate five hours to selected buyer conversations, five to a reusable technical demonstration or offer, four to paid-proposal or pilot design, three to competitive and economic research, and three to follow-up, records and contingency. These are flexible **planning values**, not an operating law.

Once a real paid engagement begins, delivery obligations dominate. A founder should not launch another pilot in a different market merely to make the research schedule look balanced. A good opportunity decision protects **the ability to fulfill existing promises**.

## 111. Market choice and the three-bucket institutional hypothesis

Bayes has an aspirational three-bucket architecture: a reliable educational core; scalable partnerships and service work; and patient, high-variance research or intellectual-property opportunities when the institution has a stable foundation. This can be a useful **long-run allocation lens**, not a mandate to launch three lines now.

The first market should be evaluated partly on whether it produces capabilities that connect to this architecture:

- A D2C cohort may build instructional design, assessment standards and an initial educational identity.
- A college partnership may build academic integration, faculty development and regional institutional trust.
- Enterprise work may build authentic technical problems, revenue and practitioner relationships.
- Research opportunities may later reuse some of these assets, but early research should not be assumed to generate profit on demand.

The danger is declaring all cross-links inevitable. A consulting project produces reusable intellectual assets only if confidentiality permits extraction, the issue recurs, and someone documents it. A college partnership produces institutional trust only if delivery meets expectations and the relationship endures. A cohort builds reputation only if learners experience and can demonstrate real value. **The proposed flywheel must earn every arrow.**

## 112. The “global versus local” opportunity is a second-order choice

Because Bayes aspires to international relevance, it may be tempting to market a global online institute from day one. But a global audience is not one accessible market. International sales add timezone, language, payment, taxation, consumer-law, cultural, credential-expectation and faculty-scheduling issues. Global technical content can be published before global enrollment is operationally justified.

A reasonable distinction is between **global legibility** and **global commercial coverage**. Bayes can publish rigorous English-language scientific and technical work that anyone may inspect while testing a narrow India-first buyer group. Conversely, a genuinely reachable international professional segment might be viable if its purchasing system, willingness to pay, service expectations and delivery feasibility are demonstrated. The correct choice comes from market evidence and capacity, not fear that the brand will be permanently perceived as local.

A strong local network may create more value than a diffuse global collection of leads. An international niche may offer better economics than a broad local category. The relevant test is which **community of buyers and partners can be served exceptionally well now**.

## 113. The first niche is a hypothesis about learning efficiency

A narrower market permits faster learning because the buyers resemble one another in the dimensions that matter. If ten participants all face the task of evaluating production AI systems, their recurring difficulties may inform reusable instruction. If ten participants range from complete beginners to research scientists, a generic course may produce a confused signal and excessive support costs.

However, narrowing can be taken too far. An invented segment defined by seventeen demographic attributes may be too small to finance a business. The useful niche is one whose members share an **economically consequential job and purchasing mechanism**, are identifiable, and can be served with a coherent promise.

The first niche is therefore not the final identity of Bayes. It is the smallest market in which the institute can test and learn the capabilities it hopes eventually to reproduce at scale.

## 114. What Bayes should conclude now

The evidence available today does **not** justify the statement “Bayes's best first market is definitely D2C,” “college partnerships are guaranteed revenue,” or “enterprise AI training offers the highest ROI.” Each has plausible advantages, and each contains important unknowns. What the analysis *does* justify is a selection principle:

> Prefer the next opportunity in which Bayes can reach an actual buyer, make a bounded promise within founder capacity, obtain a meaningful paid commitment, verify real value, retain acceptable economics, and create an asset useful for the future institution.

If a college can authorize a well-defined paid pilot sooner than a D2C cohort can enroll, it may be rational to do the college pilot. If employed engineers pay for a small reviewed studio and show strong learning evidence while colleges remain stalled in approvals, D2C may deserve priority. If an enterprise sponsor has an urgent, specific problem and funds a bounded diagnostic with acceptable terms, that may be an excellent first engagement. The market, not the founder's preference for one business-model label, should decide the **order of experiments**.

This is more demanding than picking a niche from a list. It also respects the distinction between an institute's enduring purpose and its changing commercial entry strategy.

## 118. A 45-minute market discovery conversation

Start with a recent actual event, not your product idea. The goal is to reconstruct behavior:

**Opening (5 minutes):** “Tell me about the last time this problem became important enough that you considered a solution. What happened?”

**Consequences and alternatives (10 minutes):** “What was at stake? What had you tried? What would have happened if you had done nothing? Which alternatives did you actually evaluate?”

**The transaction (10 minutes):** “Who approved the purchase? What budget did it come from? What did you spend or ask others to spend? What objections or procedural steps almost stopped it?”

**Dissatisfaction and workaround (10 minutes):** “What remained unsolved? What extra work did you or your team have to do? Can you describe or show the workflow, without disclosing confidential material?”

**Market and referrals (5 minutes):** “Who else faces a similar situation? Who would you ask for a recommendation? Which provider do you trust and why?”

**Close (5 minutes):** “What important detail have I missed? If I later build a small, clearly described solution to this particular job, would you be comfortable reviewing the actual scope?”

If a research conversation is genuinely not a sales conversation, do not disguise a sales pitch as research. Record actions, timelines, alternatives and objections rather than asking people to predict how much they might admire your future company. Chapter 6 will deepen this method.

## 119. A practical 30-day market investigation

A founder with limited resources can conduct a first rigorous pass within a month, though complex industries need longer periods.

**Days 1–5:** Write up to six distinct buyer–job–purchase hypotheses. Eliminate those violating legal, mission, capital or deliverability boundaries. For each remaining hypothesis, map current alternatives and the buyer's likely trigger.

**Days 6–12:** Collect public research, name possible buyer accounts and decision-makers, and estimate bottom-up market potential with ranges. Inspect rival offers and value chains. Record assumptions and sources.

**Days 13–20:** Conduct focused discovery conversations with recent actual buyers and appropriate budget holders. Reconstruct spending, workarounds and purchasing friction. Reassess whether your market boundary is correct.

**Days 21–26:** Design a **small complete offer** for the most plausible opportunity. Estimate honest direct costs, acquisition effort, delivery capacity and cash. Seek a real paid commitment or appropriate costly action, subject to feasible truthful delivery.

**Days 27–30:** Write a market-selection memo with supported and unsupported claims. Choose one next experiment. Defer the others, maintaining their evidence records rather than launching them all simultaneously.

A month of research cannot prove a long-term market. It can prevent avoidable errors and reveal where serious experimentation should begin.

## 120. The opportunity-selection decision tree

Follow the branches in order:

**A. Can you identify a concrete buyer and consequential job?** If not, investigate before building.

**B. Is there evidence of current expenditure, costly workarounds or serious behavioral commitment?** If not, test demand without a large product investment.

**C. Is the commercial transaction possible within legal, budgetary and institutional constraints?** If not, change buyer, payer or market layer—or defer.

**D. Can your team reach enough buyers at credible acquisition cost?** If not, find better access, a narrower segment, or a partner with aligned incentives.

**E. Can you deliver an improved result within current capacity and earn sustainable contribution?** If not, redesign service, price, operations or customer segment.

**F. Does the opportunity survive a realistic competitive response, at least long enough to earn customers and learn?** If not, identify a more defensible operating position.

**G. Does the opportunity fit your downside limit and produce strategically useful options?** If yes, run the next bounded paid experiment; if not, defer or select a different opportunity.

The tree does not tell you which product to build. It tells you **what must be true before choosing to build it**.

## 121. Twelve common market-selection mistakes

**Mistake 1: Choosing by TAM alone.** A percentage of a large number is not a customer-acquisition plan.

**Mistake 2: Confusing need with demand.** People can benefit from something they will never buy.

**Mistake 3: Treating the entire industry as a customer segment.** One broad category can contain incompatible jobs and payers.

**Mistake 4: Ignoring self-production and inaction.** An internal workaround may be a more important competitor than the famous vendor.

**Mistake 5: Interpreting no competitors as a gift.** It may signal no budget, no need, or impossibly hard adoption.

**Mistake 6: Interpreting many competitors as proof of an opening.** It proves some demand exists, not that the entrant can survive the economics.

**Mistake 7: Assuming high growth yields high profit.** New entrants, platforms and suppliers may capture the expanding value.

**Mistake 8: Measuring revenue where you should measure profit and cash.** A high-ticket contract can destroy runway or founder capacity.

**Mistake 9: Confusing a meeting or memorandum with payment.** Commercial demand requires a buying mechanism, not enthusiasm alone.

**Mistake 10: Treating the founder's expertise as universal market access.** Being able to build or teach does not imply procurement, distribution or credibility.

**Mistake 11: Treating a market score as scientific truth.** Compensating scores can disguise fatal constraints.

**Mistake 12: Refusing to revise the definition of the market.** The best opportunity may be a different payer, buyer situation, product layer or niche than the founder first imagined.

## 122. The selection thesis: one paragraph worth defending

After an investigation, the founder should be able to write:

> We believe **[precise buyer]** in **[circumstance]** experiences **[valuable job and cost of failure]**. Today they use **[actual alternatives]**, which leave **[specific costly gap]** because **[structural explanation]**. We can reach them through **[verified path]** and offer **[bounded solution]** at **[price and payment terms]** with **[plausible full-cost economics]**. The opportunity exists now because **[timing mechanism]**. Our starting advantage is **[real capability or access]**; the greatest threats are **[supplier, rival, substitute, or customer behavior]**. We will invest no more than **[affordable loss]** before testing **[decisive assumption]**, and we will change course if **[falsifying evidence]** appears.

This is the output of Chapter 5. It is not a product specification, positioning statement, sales funnel or full business plan. Those follow. It is **a reasoned argument that this particular market is worth investigating with scarce founder resources**.

# Epilogue — The difference between a promising world and a promising business

Most great companies begin in worlds that could support many possible businesses. The world is full of inefficient work, incomplete learning, inconvenient transactions, underserved people, and scientific possibilities. It is easy to admire these opportunities in the abstract. The difficulty is finding where need becomes a decision, where a decision becomes a transaction, and where the transaction can be fulfilled profitably and repeatedly by **this organization**.

The founder who sees a crowded category and runs away may miss a profitable segment. The founder who sees an empty category and rushes in may discover there is no market. The founder who sees a huge report and buys advertising may have mistaken other people's revenue for accessible demand. The founder who learns the exact buyer, budget, alternative, constraint, profit bottleneck and timing has a more durable advantage: **a better understanding of the game before committing to play it**.

An excellent market is not a substitute for execution. A brilliant team can sometimes reshape a difficult market. Luck and change matter. But a disciplined market-selection process improves the probability that the founder's scarce effort is being applied to something that can become valuable—and makes mistakes cheaper to discover.

> **Do not begin by asking how much of a giant market you can capture. Begin by asking which particular buyer has a problem worth solving, why that buyer will choose to act, which part of the economic system will pay you, and whether success will make the next transaction easier rather than harder.**

The next chapter, *The Theory of Customer Understanding*, moves from the structure of the market into the lived decisions of its participants. We will stop treating “customers” as cells in a spreadsheet and study the events, trade-offs, fears, incentives and behaviors by which real purchases happen.

## The theory in fourteen principles

1. **A market is a system of buyer decisions and exchanges, not a count of people who could benefit.**
2. **Need, interest, willingness to pay, ability to pay and actual purchase are different facts.**
3. **Define a market at the level of a buyer, job, trigger, alternatives and budget before broadening it.**
4. **Substitutes, self-production and inaction are as important as direct rivals.**
5. **Industry growth does not imply profit growth or an accessible entrant position.**
6. **Market structure distributes value among buyers, sellers, suppliers and complements.**
7. **Map where profit and bargaining power lie, not only where headline revenue sits.**
8. **Treat TAM as a bounded hypothesis and SOM as an acquisition-and-capacity mechanism.**
9. **A gap is a costly unsatisfied job whose economics can support a supplier—not an empty feature cell.**
10. **Entry barriers, exit barriers and the incumbent's rational response determine whether an opening persists.**
11. **Timing is the alignment of technology, customers, complements, institutions and founder readiness.**
12. **The founder's ability to reach and serve a market matters as much as the market's abstract attractiveness.**
13. **Select small experiments that change decisions before committing to large, irreversible business models.**
14. **The best initial market lets the founder create real value, capture enough to survive, learn quickly, and accumulate capabilities relevant to the business they hope to build.**
