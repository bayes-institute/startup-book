\section{A Rational Founder Does Not Try to Eliminate Luck}

\subsection{Staged Commitments and Asymmetric Experiments}

The existence of luck can produce 2 opposite mistakes. Some founders conclude that success is mostly random and therefore planning is pointless. Others cannot tolerate uncertainty and create elaborate plans that quietly assume their forecasts will come true.

The right response is different: design a sequence of decisions that benefits from learning and does not require early certainty.

Suppose you believe a particular customer segment needs technical training. You could spend 2 years designing a complete platform, recruiting a large staff, leasing a campus, and buying advertising. Or you could spend several weeks speaking to potential buyers, show them a concrete proposed outcome, deliver a demanding sample workshop, invite a small number to make a real purchase, and measure whether the result is worth repeating.

The 2nd path is not automatically superior for every industry. You cannot discover the efficacy of a new drug with a weekend prototype. You cannot test a nuclear reactor by charging 10 early customers. Scientific research and regulated industries sometimes require long, expensive experiments before a product exists. Even there, staged commitments, intermediate evidence, and carefully chosen partnerships can reduce the cost of uncertainty.

The point is that a founder should match the size of a commitment to the quality of the evidence and the irreversibility of the decision.

Small, reversible bets are useful early. Larger investments become rational when they purchase capabilities or market access that cannot be acquired cheaply in another way. A decision to hire a specialist for a 3-month project is different from signing a long office lease. Building a prototype is different from purchasing land. Accepting a small client contract is different from promising thousands of students an outcome over several years.

Founders sometimes invoke boldness to justify the largest possible action. \textbf{Ambitious people need not maximize the size of their first bet. Often the wiser approach is to maximize how much can be learned per unit of irreversible cost.}

If the 1st idea fails, the founder who has accumulated cash, knowledge, relationships, and goodwill may be better positioned than when they began. The founder who borrowed heavily, made exaggerated promises, and destroyed trust may be worse positioned even if they learned just as much. Both may call their experience a failure. Economically, the outcomes are quite different.
