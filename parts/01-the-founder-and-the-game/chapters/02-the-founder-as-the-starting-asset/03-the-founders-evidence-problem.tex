\section{The Founder's Evidence Problem}

\subsection{The Unreliable Witness}

People are notoriously poor witnesses to their own commercial usefulness. We remember vivid successes, discount routine competence, confuse effort with performance, and assume that what comes naturally to us must be easy for everyone else. We also mistake credentials for the ability to solve a buyer's immediate operational friction.

Self-reflection helps, but it should be the starting point of discovery rather than the concluding proof. Consider evidence in descending order of commercial relevance:

These levels are a practical convention invented for this book, not a validated psychological scale. An E5 result in an irrelevant market tells you less about a new venture than an E4 result aligned with the target problem. \textbf{Evidence relevance matters as much as nominal strength.}

\textbf{A good founder inventory always includes a column titled ``How could I be wrong?'' to prevent biography from turning into mythology.}

\subsection{3 Complementary Methods of Self-Discovery}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.},leftmargin=11pt,itemsep=1.5pt,topsep=1.5pt,parsep=0pt]
  \item \textbf{Retrospective evidence:} What have you already accomplished that other people valued?
  \item \textbf{External observation:} What do informed colleagues repeatedly notice about your decisions?
  \item \textbf{Live trials:} What happens when you attempt a bounded task under real constraints?
\end{enumerate}

Each method corrects the blind spots of the others. Retrospection is biased by selective memory. External observation can be contaminated by politeness. Live trials are noisy when the sample is small. Together they triangulate an accurate picture.
