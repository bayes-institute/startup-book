\section*{Orientation and Scope}

Chapter 23 established why hiring is an inference problem and why job analysis, structured evidence, reliability, validity, fairness, and decision rules matter. This chapter turns that science into an operating craft. The same organization may need to judge a teacher's ability to repair misconceptions, an engineer's ability to debug a production failure, a researcher's ability to reject their own result, a salesperson's ability to diagnose a buyer, and an executive's ability to allocate scarce capital. None can be selected well by a generic interview with a few technical questions added.

\textbf{Scope and evidence note.} The chapter combines established personnel-selection principles with occupationally specific \textbf{proposed assessment designs}. The latter are practical templates, not published tests with demonstrated predictive validities. Any numerical hiring scores, budgets, candidate names, returns, or Bayes Institute arrangements are \textit{illustrative and hypothetical}, not actual Bayes hires or institutional outcomes. Legal and employment requirements differ by jurisdiction; obtain appropriate local advice for consequential decisions. In particular, assessment tasks should be job-related, accessible, proportionate, transparently administered, and handled with candidate consent and data care.
