\section*{Part II: Where Valuable Differences Actually Come From}

\subsection*{11. Start with a Job, Not an Industry Label}

A category label tells you how suppliers classify their products. A job-to-be-done describes the progress a customer seeks in a particular circumstance. ``AI course'' describes supply. ``I have 12 weeks to ship a reliable AI feature and cannot tell whether my design is sound'' describes a consequential situation.

A useful job statement specifies \textbf{actor, trigger, desired progress, constraint, current alternative, and cost of failure}. It should also recognize emotional and social stakes. A manager implementing software may want faster work and less personal blame. A learner may want capability and relief from fear of professional obsolescence. A parent buying a product may want convenience and confidence that they are caring responsibly for their family.

Jobs are not magic words. Founders still need evidence that the described situation occurs often enough, has a route to payment, and can be improved. A poetic restatement of a vague desire is not market discovery. The practical task is to reconstruct real episodes of purchasing or working around a problem, as Chapter 6 developed.

Other mechanisms can be nested within these: personalization, reliability, customization, financing, local presence, safety, ethical standards, continuity, interoperability, and specialist expertise. The goal is not to activate every row. \textbf{The goal is to identify 2 or 3 levers that reinforce the same customer's job and can be supported by 1 coherent operating model.}
