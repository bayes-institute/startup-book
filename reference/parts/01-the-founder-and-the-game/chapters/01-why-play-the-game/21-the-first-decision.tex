\section{The First Decision}

\subsection{What the Business Produces in the Founder}

There is one last distinction to make. People sometimes imagine a career as a sequence of external achievements: a higher salary, a more impressive title, a larger company, an investment round, a valuation, a famous office, or a campus. Such markers can provide useful information, but they are also dangerously easy to optimize independently of the underlying goal.

A founder who wants to become wealthy may be better served by a boring, profitable company than a fashionable startup. A founder who wants to solve an important scientific problem may need to spend years building credibility rather than revenue. A founder who wants to create a great school may need to refuse opportunities that would produce short-term enrollment at the cost of learning quality. A founder who wants autonomy may need to avoid funding structures that require a particular kind of scale.

To understand a business, ask what it is producing. To understand the founder, ask what the business is producing in the founder.

Does it develop better judgment? Does it create the freedom the founder hoped for, or more dependence? Does it make them more intellectually honest, or more attached to defending an old hypothesis? Does it build the capacity to work with extraordinary people? Does it make promises they are proud to keep? Does it gradually create assets and relationships that make the next generation of work possible?

\textbf{A business that earns money while slowly destroying the founder's curiosity, relationships, or integrity may have an impressive financial statement and a poor total return.} Conversely, a company that fails commercially can still create valuable knowledge and relationships, although those benefits should not be invoked as a retrospective justification for taking reckless risks. Not every failure is a disguised success.

The purpose of these questions is not to moralize about wanting money. It is to recognize that money is usually sought because of what it makes possible. If a founder forgets that, wealth can become another treadmill rather than an escape from one.

\subsection{The Synthesis of Ambition and Reality}

At the beginning of this chapter, two equally capable people faced different possible futures. It would be comforting to finish by announcing that the entrepreneur is the winner. But that would make the chapter less useful.

The employee may gain financial independence and do work of great importance. The founder may build a valuable enterprise, or may discover that the problem they chose was not worth what it cost them. One might change paths several times. The most intelligent choice may even be to remain employed until a particular opportunity has been validated and the founder has enough resources to pursue it without intolerable pressure.

There is nevertheless a reason entrepreneurship keeps attracting people who could have comfortable careers elsewhere. A company is an unusual vehicle for converting judgment into assets, for mobilizing other people's capabilities, and for turning a solution to one person's problem into something that can help thousands or millions. When the economics work, the person who owns part of that vehicle may share in the value produced long after the original work was done. When the institution works, the capability to create value may even outlive the people who founded it.

This possibility explains the attraction. It does not guarantee the result.

\textbf{If you choose to become a founder, the important question is not whether you can work harder than everyone else. Nor whether you can imagine a company worth a billion dollars. It is whether you can discover a worthwhile problem, assemble the means to solve it, earn a sustainable share of the value created, preserve enough resources to survive mistakes, and build capabilities that compound.}

And before any of those questions comes an even more personal one: What is worth spending a substantial part of my life building?

\textbf{A person who can answer that question has not yet found a business. But they have begun to choose the game they are willing to play.}

The next chapter begins from there. Before studying markets, competitors, or product ideas, we must understand the most important resource the founder possesses at the start: the founder's own particular combination of capabilities, knowledge, relationships, resources, and constraints.
