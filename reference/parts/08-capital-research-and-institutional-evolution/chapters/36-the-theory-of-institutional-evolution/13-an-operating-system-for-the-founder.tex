\subsection{115. Weekly: learn whether the business is alive}

A 45-minute weekly review should cover cash in bank (reconciled), cash due in the next 13 weeks, live customer commitments, quality incidents, teacher workload, sales pipeline, and the week's single most important bottleneck. Keep the dashboard short enough to use consistently. Monthly review can be more detailed.

The founder should also spend direct time each week with learners or customers. It prevents strategic planning from floating away from actual value creation.

In addition to financial accounts, document what the organization \textit{learned and retained}.

\subsection{117. Quarterly: capital allocation and expansion committee}

At 1st this can be the founder, a financially literate adviser, and a respected educator. Later it should become a formal governance process. Review product P\&Ls, actual outcomes, portfolio concentration, cash stress, new-investment memos, hiring needs, and option budgets. Explicitly ask: ``What is the best use of the next rupee and the next founder hour?''

Reserve the right to change the plan. A strategic plan is a hypothesis portfolio, not a contract with the future.

\subsection{118. Annually: publish a report worth believing}

An institutional annual report should include number of learners, admissions and completion definitions, verified capability evidence, employment outcomes only where measured and appropriately attributed, financial highlights, scholarships, faculty development, research artifacts, notable mistakes and corrections, conflicts-management disclosures, and next-year priorities.

Do not publish fabricated placement statistics, selected-only success stories as population averages, or unverified causal claims. Some years the most valuable finding will be a failed teaching experiment and an honest explanation of how Bayes responded.

\subsection{119. The founder should have 2 scoreboards}

\textbf{Commercial scoreboard:} financial survival, revenue quality, contribution margins, customer trust, and managerial leverage.

\textbf{Institutional scoreboard:} learner capability, faculty standards, technical knowledge produced, academic relationships, governance, donor confidence, and independence from founding personalities.

The 2nd scoreboard helps prevent the 1st from swallowing the mission. The 1st prevents the 2nd from becoming an unfunded vision.

\subsection{120. The founder's changing psychological contract}

In the 1st years, the founder must tolerate anonymity and manual work. Later, the founder must tolerate the discomfort of delegating to people who do things differently. Later still, the founder must accept that worthy academic leaders may disagree with company strategy or refuse to make scientific claims for commercial reasons.

Entrepreneurial courage at 0 means asking strangers to pay. At \textnormal{₹}5 crore it means empowering competent leaders. At \textnormal{₹}50 crore it means refusing the seductive bad acquisition. At a university's founding it may mean giving independent scholars authority the founder cannot override. These are different strengths.

The founder must learn to exchange \textbf{being the best person in the room} for \textbf{making the room capable of sustained excellence}.
